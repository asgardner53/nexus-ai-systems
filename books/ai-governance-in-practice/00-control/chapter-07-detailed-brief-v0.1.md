# Chapter 7 Detailed Brief v0.1 — Capability Before Compliance

**Book:** *AI Governance in Practice*  
**Author:** Alec Gardner  
**Date:** 2026-11-18  
**Status:** Detailed chapter brief locked for drafting  
**Target length:** 2,700–3,200 words

## Chapter role in the book

Chapter 6 established that governance becomes operational through the infrastructure of work: data, identities, permissions, workflows, configurations, documentation, logs, suppliers and lifecycle controls. It ended with a question that infrastructure cannot answer on its own:

> **Can the people carrying accountability recognise when the control environment is no longer enough?**

Chapter 7 must show that capability is not an auxiliary training activity performed after governance has been designed. It is part of the mechanism through which governance works.

The chapter moves the argument from operational controls to human and organisational judgement. It must explain why an organisation can possess:

- approved policies;
- technically implemented controls;
- documented roles;
- high training-completion rates;
- accessible escalation channels;

and still lack the practical capability to recognise, challenge and respond to an AI-related problem.

The chapter's task is not to produce a curriculum or workforce-development plan. It is to make one organisational shift unmistakable:

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

The supporting distinction is:

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

These are Gardner synthesis informed by established evidence. They are not presented as external definitions, a validated capability model or a certification standard.

## Reader entry belief

The reader may enter the chapter believing that capability is largely established through:

- AI-awareness training;
- tool instruction;
- policy acknowledgement;
- completion records;
- access to guidance;
- named escalation routes.

A related belief is that once a person has been trained and assigned responsibility, failures of judgement are mainly individual performance failures.

The chapter must destabilise both assumptions.

Training, role assignment and guidance matter. But the ability to exercise meaningful accountability depends on whether people can use knowledge and judgement under the actual conditions of work — including uncertainty, workload, authority, incentives, system fluency, team dynamics and the response they expect when they challenge an AI-supported outcome.

## Opening tension

Open with a composite organisation whose governance dashboard is green.

The organisation has introduced an AI-supported case-prioritisation system. It may be used in operations, claims, customer service, workforce allocation or another cross-sector setting, but avoid duplicating the customer-service assistant in Chapter 5 or the supplier-onboarding system in Chapter 6. A suitable setting is a **service-demand prioritisation workflow** affecting which cases receive urgent attention.

The dashboard shows:

- 98 or 99 per cent completion of mandatory AI training;
- every relevant manager has acknowledged the oversight procedure;
- the system limitation is documented;
- uncertainty indicators are visible;
- an escalation path exists;
- managers possess authority to pause or redirect a case;
- no material incident has been recorded.

During a realistic tabletop exercise, participants receive a fluent, plausible AI-supported recommendation. The recommendation is not obviously absurd. It contains a material weakness that becomes visible only when several signals are combined:

- one important data field is missing or stale;
- the uncertainty indicator is elevated but not extreme;
- the case falls near an escalation threshold;
- the AI explanation sounds confident and coherent;
- the operational team is under time pressure;
- the system has performed reliably in recent cases.

The participants proceed.

One employee senses that the recommendation does not fit the circumstances but assumes the system has already been approved and does not want to delay the queue. The manager has completed the required training but cannot explain what the uncertainty signal means or whether it triggers escalation. A second employee treats the explanation as corroboration rather than as an output generated by the same system. The formal intervention route exists, but nobody uses it.

The exercise ends before harm occurs. The governance dashboard remains green.

Opening question:

> **What exactly did the organisation believe the training record proved?**

The organisation and exercise must be identified lightly as composite. Do not imply a documented real incident.

## Dominant proposition

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

Supporting proposition:

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

The chapter must immediately preserve the boundary that compliance records, training and role documentation remain necessary. The problem is not that these controls are worthless. The problem is that they answer a different question from demonstrated operational capability.

## Primary reader discovery

The reader should discover that capability is not stored in a learning-management system or possessed permanently by an individual. It emerges from the interaction of:

- AI literacy;
- domain and task knowledge;
- critical evaluation;
- calibrated reliance;
- practice and feedback;
- work design;
- retained professional judgement;
- role clarity and authority;
- psychological permission to challenge;
- usable escalation and intervention;
- collective expertise;
- organisational response.

The chapter should leave the reader with a different question. Instead of asking whether people have been trained, the reader should ask:

> **What must these people be able to recognise, judge and do in the work for the governance arrangement to remain real?**

## Terminology and conceptual boundaries

### AI literacy

Use **AI literacy** in its established broad sense: competencies supporting recognition, understanding, use, critical evaluation, collaboration and ethical awareness.

Do not reduce literacy to prompt-writing or tool operation.

Do not equate literacy with role-specific operational competence.

### Capability

Use **capability** as ordinary organisational language rather than a named Gardner framework.

In this chapter, capability means the practical and maintainable ability of a person, team or accountable arrangement to recognise when judgement is required, interpret relevant evidence, evaluate AI-supported work, challenge it appropriately and use intervention or escalation mechanisms in context.

Do not present this definition as a validated external competency model.

### Compliance

Compliance includes relevant obligations, required training, records, procedures and evidence that an organisation has performed specified activities.

Do not caricature compliance as useless bureaucracy. The chapter's distinction is between:

- evidence that an activity occurred; and
- evidence that the required judgement and action remain available in practice.

### Trust and reliance

Prefer **appropriate reliance** or **calibrated reliance** over the vague aim of “building trust”.

The objective is not maximum trust, maximum scepticism or routine human override. It is a workable alignment between reliance and the actual capability and limits of the system in the relevant task.

### Expertise and deskilling

Do not use deskilling as an inevitable consequence of AI.

The evidence supports several possible paths:

- augmentation;
- redistribution of cognitive work;
- faster development for less experienced workers;
- reduction in opportunities to practise some tasks;
- weakened scrutiny under overconfidence;
- retention or erosion of expertise depending on work design.

## Narrative movement

### Movement 1 — The dashboard says capability is complete

Develop the tabletop exercise without explaining it too quickly.

Let the reader see an apparently well-controlled organisation:

- high completion rates;
- current policy acknowledgements;
- named oversight roles;
- visible system documentation;
- no open incident.

Then reveal the team's failure to use the available signals and controls.

The key tension is not ignorance of the policy. The team has been informed. The failure lies in the gap between receiving information and being able to use it under pressure.

Avoid blaming one employee. The exercise should expose a system of conditions:

- a fluent recommendation;
- ambiguous evidence;
- queue pressure;
- high recent reliability;
- unclear escalation thresholds;
- manager uncertainty;
- perceived social cost of slowing the work.

### Movement 2 — What a training record proves

Move from the scene to the distinction between participation and performance.

A training record can demonstrate that:

- the person was assigned the module;
- they completed it;
- they may have passed an associated knowledge check;
- the organisation communicated a requirement.

It cannot, by itself, demonstrate that the person can:

- recognise a live boundary condition;
- interpret uncertainty;
- integrate domain evidence;
- resist an authoritative-looking output;
- formulate an alternative judgement;
- escalate at the right time;
- intervene despite operational pressure.

Use training-transfer research to distinguish learning from generalisation and maintenance in work. The chapter should acknowledge that effective training can make a material difference. Its target is **training-as-proof**, not training itself.

Introduce the supporting proposition after the reader has experienced the gap:

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

### Movement 3 — Literacy is necessary and incomplete

Introduce AI-literacy evidence through Long and Magerko and Ng and colleagues.

Show that literacy includes more than tool use:

- recognising AI;
- understanding relevant capabilities and limitations;
- using and collaborating with AI;
- evaluating outputs;
- considering ethical and social implications.

Connect this to Article 4 of the EU AI Act and current Australian guidance, with jurisdictional qualification.

Then establish the organisational boundary. A person may understand that an AI system can be uncertain, biased or wrong yet still lack the domain knowledge to recognise a particular error or the authority to change the decision.

The chapter should state plainly:

> AI literacy gives people a language for the problem. Operational capability determines whether they can do anything useful with it.

Use this as Gardner prose, not a named doctrine.

### Movement 4 — Domain judgement remains part of the control

Return to the tabletop case.

The material weakness was not visible through generic AI awareness alone. It required knowledge of the service context, the missing information, the consequence of delayed attention and the reason the recommendation did not fit the whole case.

Explain that oversight often depends on complementary knowledge:

- the AI can process patterns at scale;
- the human may understand context, purpose, exceptions and consequences;
- a technical specialist may understand system behaviour;
- a manager may understand authority and risk appetite;
- another role may understand legal, professional or stakeholder obligations.

Capability may therefore be distributed. One person need not know everything, but the accountable arrangement must assemble the necessary expertise reliably and in time.

Do not imply that only senior experts should use AI or that human expertise is infallible.

### Movement 5 — The real objective is calibrated reliance

Introduce the human-factors literature on trust, misuse, disuse, automation bias and calibrated reliance.

The participants in the exercise do not simply “trust AI too much”. Their reliance is shaped by:

- recent system reliability;
- the fluent explanation;
- ambiguity in the case;
- workload;
- uncertainty about escalation;
- perceived cost of delay;
- assumptions about prior approval.

Explain that capability involves several possible responses:

- accept;
- verify;
- seek more evidence;
- challenge;
- defer;
- override;
- escalate;
- stop.

The correct response depends on task, consequence and available evidence.

Use Zhang, Liao and Bellamy and Buçinca and colleagues to show that explanations and cognitive forcing do not offer a magic solution. Explanations may help, fail or increase confidence. Friction may improve scrutiny but create delay or effort. The governance challenge is to design for appropriate reliance, not to maximise scepticism.

### Movement 6 — Human plus AI is not automatically better

Use Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone's meta-analysis as the main challenge to easy claims of “best of both worlds”.

State the evidence carefully:

- 106 experiments;
- 370 effect sizes;
- human-AI combinations did not, on average, outperform the better of human or AI alone;
- task type and relative human/AI capability mattered;
- decision tasks and creation tasks showed different patterns.

The organisational lesson is not to choose human-only or AI-only by default. It is to design the division of labour and test the combined arrangement.

Tie this back to Chapters 4 and 5 without repeating the Meaningful Accountability Test or Delegated Authority Map. The organisation must know what judgement it expects humans to add and whether the work design still allows them to add it.

### Movement 7 — AI can build capability and erode it

Introduce the productivity evidence from Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond.

Use the verified details:

- 5,172 customer-support agents;
- 15 per cent average productivity increase;
- larger gains among less experienced and lower-skilled workers.

This prevents the chapter from becoming anti-AI or romanticising unaided expertise. AI can make good practice more available and improve performance.

Then bring in Lee and colleagues' survey of 319 knowledge workers and 936 examples. Explain the reported shift in cognitive work:

- gathering to verification;
- problem-solving to response integration;
- task execution to stewardship.

Higher AI confidence was associated with lower self-reported critical-thinking effort. Preserve the limits: association, self-report, younger technology-skilled sample and no proof of inevitable decline.

The chapter's argument is that capability effects depend on design. If AI performs more routine work, the organisation must decide:

- which judgement people still need;
- how novices will develop it;
- how experts will maintain it;
- what practice and feedback remain;
- how people learn from exceptions and errors;
- whether the organisation is producing augmentation or dependence.

Do not introduce a new “augmentation–deskilling continuum” or scoring model.

### Movement 8 — Managers need a different capability

Shift from front-line users to managers and accountable roles.

The manager in the tabletop exercise has completed training but cannot interpret the operational meaning of the uncertainty signal or identify when the process should pause.

Manager capability includes being able to:

- identify where AI participates in the work;
- understand the authority delegated to it;
- know what judgement remains human;
- interpret relevant performance and risk signals;
- recognise when staff are being given impossible oversight duties;
- protect escalation and pause decisions;
- align workload, incentives and resources with the expected controls;
- know when specialist expertise is required;
- distinguish a user error from a design or governance failure.

Use NIST, the EU AI Act and Australian guidance visibly. Use Jöhnk and colleagues for the wider organisational-readiness context.

Preserve the boundary that managers do not need to become AI engineers.

### Movement 9 — Knowing is not speaking

Return to the employee who sensed that something was wrong but remained silent.

Develop psychological safety and employee voice as governance conditions.

Use Edmondson, Detert and Burris, and Morrison to explain why speaking up involves interpersonal and organisational risk. Use O'Donovan and McAuliffe to preserve the boundary that interventions and outcomes are heterogeneous.

The chapter should distinguish:

- feeling safe to ask a question;
- believing challenge is legitimate;
- knowing how to escalate;
- believing the escalation will matter;
- possessing enough evidence and confidence to act;
- having time to interrupt the workflow.

Current Australian guidance on feedback, challenge and redress can connect voice to formal mechanisms.

State the core insight:

> Knowledge remains inert where people do not believe challenge is legitimate, safe or capable of changing the outcome.

This is a supported organisational interpretation, not a new named construct.

### Movement 10 — Capability belongs to the arrangement

Bring together individual and collective capability.

The organisation may need:

- broad literacy across the workforce;
- deeper role-specific competence for users and overseers;
- specialist expertise available on demand;
- managers who understand authority and intervention;
- risk, legal, HR, technology and operational perspectives;
- escalation pathways that assemble expertise quickly;
- feedback loops that convert exceptions into learning.

Capability should therefore be assessed at two levels:

1. what the individual role must be able to recognise and do;
2. what the accountable arrangement must be able to assemble and execute.

Preserve the Accountability–Control Gap. Do not transfer responsibility to the front-line worker for limitations created by system design, workload, vendor opacity or management incentives.

### Movement 11 — What evidence of capability looks like

Do not turn this movement into a checklist or certification scheme.

Explain that evidence should be proportionate to role and consequence. Possible evidence includes:

- scenario-based judgement;
- observation of work;
- realistic exception handling;
- interpretation of uncertainty or limitation signals;
- escalation and intervention exercises;
- work samples;
- review of decisions and corrections;
- feedback from affected functions;
- evidence of retained capability over time.

A low-consequence productivity tool may require little more than practical orientation and clear boundaries. A person overseeing consequential automated action may need repeated scenario practice and evidence that they can intervene.

Make clear that no universal validated organisational measure of AI-governance capability currently exists. Do not create a score, maturity ladder or permanent credential.

The chapter should argue for **evidence in context**, not more assessment for its own sake.

### Movement 12 — Capability before compliance

Return to the green dashboard.

The training statistic was accurate. The procedure existed. The roles were named. The organisation had not fabricated compliance.

What the dashboard could not show was whether people could:

- recognise the weak signal;
- interpret it;
- bring domain judgement to the case;
- resist fluency and recent reliability;
- challenge the recommendation;
- pause the workflow;
- escalate without unreasonable penalty.

Now state the dominant proposition:

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

The organisation's response to the exercise should not be “more training” by reflex. It may need to revise:

- the training;
- the scenario practice;
- the uncertainty display;
- the escalation threshold;
- manager expectations;
- queue targets;
- leader response;
- workload;
- access to expertise;
- feedback and learning arrangements.

This keeps the chapter organisational rather than instructional.

### Movement 13 — Handoff to Chapter 8

End by exposing a further problem.

Even capable people cannot exercise judgement if governance makes action excessively slow, risky, confusing or procedurally impossible. The organisation may train people to challenge AI while rewarding speed, requiring several approvals to pause a workflow or treating every exception as failure.

The next question becomes:

> **Does the governance system give capable people legitimate room to act?**

This leads directly into Chapter 8 — *Governance as Permission*.

## Evidence to appear visibly in prose

Use a disciplined selection rather than a literature parade.

### Primary and authoritative sources

- **EU AI Act Article 4** — AI literacy proportionate to knowledge, experience, training and context.
- **EU AI Act Article 26(2)** — competence, training, authority and support for oversight of defined high-risk systems.
- **NIST AI RMF Govern 2–4** — trained and empowered roles, diverse expertise, clear human-AI oversight and critical-thinking culture.
- **Australian Government Guidance for AI Adoption** — broad and role-specific literacy, lifecycle capability, failure modes, intervention, feedback and redress.

### Academic and workplace sources

- **Long & Magerko (2020)** and **Ng et al. (2021)** — AI-literacy scope.
- **Baldwin & Ford (1988)** and **Grossman & Salas (2011)** — training transfer.
- **Lee & See (2004)**; **Parasuraman & Riley (1997)**; **Parasuraman & Manzey (2010)**; **Goddard et al. (2012)** — calibrated reliance and automation bias.
- **Zhang, Liao & Bellamy (2020)** and **Buçinca et al. (2021)** — limitations and trade-offs of explanations and cognitive forcing.
- **Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024)** — human-AI performance and task dependence.
- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (2025)** — workplace augmentation and gains for less experienced workers.
- **Lee et al. (2025)** — self-reported critical-thinking effort and cognitive-work shifts.
- **Zhang et al. (2025)** — workplace AI-training needs, validation, rights and grievance.
- **Jöhnk, Weißert & Wyrtki (2020/2021)** — organisational AI readiness.
- **Edmondson (1999)**; **Detert & Burris (2007)**; **Morrison (2014)**; **O'Donovan & McAuliffe (2020)** — psychological safety, voice and intervention boundaries.

## Evidence that should remain in the background unless required

Do not overload the chapter with:

- complete AI-literacy competency lists;
- education-sector curriculum studies;
- detailed learning-transfer taxonomies;
- generic change-management models;
- sector-specific healthcare deskilling commentary;
- self-report AI-competency scales;
- proposed certification systems;
- technical prompt-engineering instruction;
- a complete workforce-skills strategy;
- detailed assessment design reserved for a later implementation tool.

Recent studies on agentic-AI readiness, AI dependence and manager capability remain in the monitoring record unless they materially improve a claim before publication.

## Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes

- AI literacy includes understanding, use, evaluation and ethical awareness;
- training must generalise and persist in work to affect capability;
- reliance should be calibrated and is context-dependent;
- explanations do not reliably produce correct reliance;
- human-AI combinations are not automatically synergistic;
- AI can improve workplace performance and benefit less experienced workers in some settings;
- generative AI may change cognitive effort and scrutiny;
- psychological safety and leader openness support voice;
- current regulation and guidance require role-appropriate literacy, competence, authority, support and challenge mechanisms.

### Reasonable interpretation

- capability is role-specific, situated, maintained and often collective;
- training records are incomplete evidence of governance capability;
- organisations must preserve the judgement on which accountability depends;
- manager capability is distinct from ordinary tool proficiency;
- challenge requires knowledge, voice, channels, authority and organisational response;
- evidence of capability should be connected to real work and proportionate consequence.

### Gardner synthesis

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

## Claims and wording controls

### Safe claims

- AI literacy is necessary for many roles but does not itself prove operational competence.
- Training completion and learning transfer are different outcomes.
- Appropriate reliance is more useful than maximum trust.
- Explanations and confidence scores do not consistently improve human-AI performance.
- AI can augment, redistribute or weaken human expertise depending on work design and context.
- Human-AI combinations do not automatically outperform the better of human or AI alone.
- Managers need role-specific capability to govern human-AI work, escalation and intervention.
- Psychological safety supports challenge but does not guarantee detection or voice.
- Capability can be distributed across a team or governance arrangement.
- Capability evidence should be proportionate and connected to performance in context.

### Prohibited or unsafe claims

Do not state or imply that:

- AI inevitably deskills people;
- training does not work;
- course completion is meaningless;
- AI literacy guarantees responsible use;
- explanations eliminate automation bias;
- human oversight always improves outcomes;
- psychological safety guarantees speaking up;
- individual workers should compensate for poor system design;
- every manager must become technically expert;
- a scenario exercise permanently certifies competence;
- the chapter presents a validated capability model.

## Chapter boundary

Chapter 7 owns:

- literacy versus operational competence;
- training transfer and practice;
- domain judgement and calibrated reliance;
- augmentation, cognitive redistribution and potential expertise erosion;
- manager and accountable-role capability;
- collective capability;
- voice, escalation confidence and psychological permission;
- evidence of capability in work;
- capability as a governance mechanism.

Chapter 7 should not fully develop:

- enabling versus coercive formalisation, proportionality and authorised room to act — Chapter 8;
- assurance records and the Evolved Governance Chain — Chapter 9;
- advanced agent supervision and runtime capability — Chapter 10;
- a detailed training curriculum, competency standard or assessment instrument.

## Book-prose controls

- Use sustained narrative rather than a sequence of competency headings.
- Keep the tabletop exercise active across the chapter.
- Avoid a generic “skills organisations need” list.
- Do not sound like a learning-and-development handbook.
- Introduce evidence where it changes the reader's understanding rather than collecting citations in blocks.
- Preserve the tension between augmentation and erosion.
- Keep responsibility institutional: capability failures may originate in work design, incentives, workload, interfaces, leadership or system assumptions.
- Avoid excessive short emphatic paragraphs.
- Australian English throughout.
