## Evidence surviving correctly

### Endsley & Kiris (1995)

The manuscript uses the study for the bounded proposition that higher automation can reduce situation awareness and impair effective takeover following automation failure.

The manuscript preserves the limitation that automation does not always weaken judgement and that the effect should not be universalised across all AI contexts.

**Outcome:** PASS.

### European Union AI Act

The manuscript accurately uses the AI Act as a jurisdiction-specific regulatory mirror. It reflects Article 14 requirements concerning understanding system capabilities and limits, monitoring, automation bias, interpretation, override and intervention, and deployer obligations to appoint people with competence, training, authority and support.

The manuscript explicitly avoids presenting the EU requirements as a universal global blueprint.

**Outcome:** PASS.

### OECD AI Principles

The manuscript accurately connects accountability to an actor's role, context and ability to act, together with traceability and ongoing lifecycle risk management.

This evidence supports distributed responsibility without implying equal responsibility among all actors.

**Outcome:** PASS.

### Elish (2019)

The moral crumple zone is represented accurately as the possible misattribution of responsibility to a nearby human actor who had limited practical control over an automated system.

The manuscript preserves the boundary that not every human reviewer is a moral crumple zone.

**Outcome:** PASS.

### NIST AI RMF

The manuscript accurately states that NIST requires clear roles, responsibilities and delegated authorities while placing responsibility for AI-risk decisions with executive leadership.

The Gardner movement from this evidence to “Delegation does not transfer accountability” remains visibly interpretive rather than a quoted NIST rule.

**Outcome:** PASS.

### MIT CISR (2026)

The manuscript accurately describes the distinction among framing, acting and learning and the need to vary human/AI decision rights according to risk and ambiguity while maintaining clear human accountability.

The source is treated as current practitioner research rather than universal empirical law.

**Outcome:** PASS.

## Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes

- automation can reduce situation awareness and impair takeover performance in relevant contexts;
- effective oversight regimes assume understanding, monitoring, interpretation, override and intervention capability;
- accountability is role- and context-dependent;
- responsibility can be misattributed to a human with limited control;
- organisations need explicit roles, delegated authorities and executive responsibility for AI risk;
- decision rights can be distributed across framing, acting and learning.

### Reasonable interpretation

- formal human presence may coexist with weak practical control;
- human sign-off can overstate the quality of independent judgement;
- distributed responsibility requires legible organisational accountability;
- a governance dashboard can establish that approval occurred without establishing that meaningful control was available.

### Gardner synthesis

> **Delegation does not transfer accountability.**

> **Accountability–Control Gap:** the mismatch between accountability assigned to a person, role or organisation and the practical control they can realistically exercise over the decision or outcome.

The manuscript makes the provenance boundary visible and does not imply external validation of the Gardner terminology.
