# Chapter 1 Forensic Evidence Survival + Developmental Review

**Chapter:** The AI You Are Already Governing  
**Manuscript reviewed:** v0.1  
**Date:** 2026-11-01  
**Overall decision:** **SUBSTANTIAL REVISION REQUIRED**

## Overall judgement

The chapter is intellectually sound and the core argument is viable. The evidence base has largely survived the move from research notes into prose, and the chapter successfully establishes the distinction between tool visibility and use-in-context.

However, it does **not** yet qualify for developmental approval because the controlled prose fails the Platinum Book-Prose Continuity gate. The manuscript relies too often on isolated one-sentence paragraphs, mini-conclusions and aphoristic transitions. The effect is readable but article-like. It resembles high-quality thought-leadership prose more than sustained book prose.

The evidence gate is **CONDITIONAL PASS**, not FAIL. Two limited evidentiary gaps require repair, but neither changes the chapter's argument.

## 1. Forensic Evidence Survival

### Evidence that survived correctly

#### Samsung case
The manuscript says Samsung restricted employee use of external generative AI after sensitive information, including source code, had been uploaded to ChatGPT.

**Finding:** supported by Bloomberg reporting based on an internal Samsung memo.

**Status:** PASS for the narrow proposition used.

#### Australian AI systems register
The manuscript states that the Australian Government register template recommends recording AI systems, use cases, accountable people and governance level, and explicitly includes AI features embedded in common software.

**Finding:** exact match to the register template.

**Status:** PASS.

#### Context-of-use proposition
The manuscript states that the same AI system can present different risks depending on use, including the comparison between marketing-email drafting and assessing job applications.

**Finding:** directly supported by the Australian Guidance for AI Adoption.

**Status:** PASS.

#### NIST context mapping
The manuscript describes NIST Map as requiring intended purpose, users, deployment setting, impacts, limitations, foreseeable uses and potential misuse, while recognising limited visibility across interdependent lifecycle actors.

**Finding:** materially supported by the NIST AI RMF and Map Playbook.

**Status:** PASS.

#### Cemex case
The manuscript describes Consult HR as operating through Teams and integrating SharePoint, ServiceNow and SAP SuccessFactors, progressing from pilot to production for approximately 800 employees.

**Finding:** supported by Microsoft customer documentation.

**Status:** PASS as an implementation illustration only.

#### Wipro case
The manuscript states that the recognition agent is embedded inside Microsoft 365 Copilot and uses enterprise data and workflows including Teams, email, OneDrive, SharePoint and related sources.

**Finding:** supported by Microsoft customer documentation.

**Status:** PASS as an implementation illustration only.

#### Proportional governance
The manuscript states that the Australian screening tool differentiates governance attention so less consequential uses do not overwhelm governance processes.

**Finding:** directly supported by the Australian screening tool, including the customer-refunds versus restaurant-booking example.

**Status:** PASS.

## 2. Limited evidence gaps

### Gap E1 — employee workarounds / routing around systems

Current text:

> “People often route around official systems because the alternative feels slower, more cumbersome or less useful.”

This is a behavioural proposition. It is plausible and consistent with shadow-IT literature, but the chapter currently provides no source.

**Required repair:** either:
- add a credible shadow-IT/shadow-AI source; or
- soften to an explicitly interpretive formulation such as “In some organisations, unauthorised use may also signal that employees find approved tools slower, less capable or harder to use.”

**Gate effect:** limited citation gap; CONDITIONAL PASS.

### Gap E2 — framing, surfacing and anchoring of human decisions

Current passage says AI can:
- frame the options a human sees;
- determine which information is surfaced or buried;
- rank candidates before review;
- generate a draft that becomes an anchor around which later judgement is made.

The first three are defensible examples of decision participation. The “anchor” claim invokes a cognitive mechanism and should not stand uncited.

**Required repair:** either:
- add relevant human-AI decision/anchoring evidence; or
- remove “anchor” as a psychological claim and write more neutrally that the generated draft “becomes the starting point for later human judgement”.

**Gate effect:** limited citation gap; CONDITIONAL PASS.

## 3. Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes
- AI governance guidance requires context-of-use understanding.
- the same system may carry different risk in different uses.
- embedded AI features should be captured in registers.
- proportional screening is appropriate.
- named enterprise cases show AI embedded in existing workflows.

### Reasonable interpretation
- a product inventory alone cannot reveal all governance-relevant consequences.
- visibility should extend to the work, decision or action influenced by AI.
- governance effort should be proportionate to consequence.

### Gardner synthesis
- **Govern the work AI participates in, not merely the tools labelled AI.**
- **Visibility without proportionality becomes bureaucracy.**
- **The first governance question is not only where AI is, but what it participates in.**

**Finding:** the evidence/synthesis boundary is generally visible and defensible.

**Theory status:** PASS.

## 4. Developmental review

### Strongest elements

1. **Opening contradiction**  
   The idea that organisations expect an identifiable “AI adoption moment” but increasingly experience no clean boundary is a strong book opening.

2. **Samsung → embedded enterprise AI progression**  
   The movement from unsanctioned use to AI hidden in ordinary enterprise infrastructure broadens the problem intelligently.

3. **Inventory versus use-in-context distinction**  
   This is the chapter's strongest conceptual movement and cleanly supports the book's wider argument.

4. **Proportionality correction**  
   The chapter avoids the trap of equating responsible governance with maximum process.

5. **Ending question**  
   “What, exactly, is governing what it is allowed to do?” genuinely creates the need for Chapter 2.

## 5. Priority revisions

### Priority 1 — Repair book-prose continuity

This is the controlling defect.

Examples of repeated short paragraph / mini-conclusion cadence include:

- “Increasingly, there is no such moment.”
- “That creates a governance problem that a simple technology inventory cannot solve on its own.”
- “The problem arises when the inventory becomes the destination rather than the starting point.”
- “Those questions begin to expose something that a software list cannot.”
- “They expose **decision participation**.”
- “There is, however, an obvious danger in taking this too far.”
- “The point is not uniformity. The point is conscious choice.”
- “Start with the work.”
- “Only then does proportional governance become possible.”
- “The organisation exists in the work people actually perform.”

Individually, several are effective. Collectively, they create a LinkedIn/HBR rhythm.

**Required repair:** rewrite these passages as fuller paragraph movements. Preserve no more than a small number of genuinely earned one-sentence paragraphs.

### Priority 2 — Reduce explicit mini-conclusions

The manuscript repeatedly explains the lesson immediately after illustrating it. Examples include:
- “The practical message is difficult to avoid...”
- “This introduces a principle...”
- “That is the real value of visibility...”
- “This suggests a different opening discipline...”

The reader often reaches the conclusion at the same moment as the prose announces it.

**Required repair:** allow the evidence and examples to accumulate for longer before naming the interpretation.

### Priority 3 — Compress the second half

The chapter makes the proportionality point more than once:
- governance overload;
- differentiated scrutiny;
- some uses under general controls;
- consequential use;
- low versus high consequence;
- visibility enabling proportional governance.

All are relevant, but the section can be shortened without losing intellectual weight.

**Required repair:** compress approximately 10–15% from the second half.

### Priority 4 — Tighten “decision participation”

The concept is useful, but its first introduction is slightly over-signposted.

**Required repair:** introduce the term inside sustained prose rather than:
> “Those questions begin to expose something... They expose **decision participation**.”

### Priority 5 — Repair E1 and E2 evidence gaps

Close the two limited evidence issues before the revised chapter can regain Evidence PASS.
