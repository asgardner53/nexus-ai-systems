## Research question

What human and organisational capabilities are required for people to exercise meaningful judgement, challenge, escalation and intervention in AI-mediated work, and how should organisations distinguish AI literacy, training completion and confidence from demonstrated operational competence?

## Chapter role

Chapter 6 established that governance becomes operational through the data, identities, permissions, workflows, configurations, records and supplier arrangements that shape what people and systems can actually do. It ended with a further problem:

> **Can the people carrying accountability recognise when the control environment is no longer enough?**

Chapter 7 must answer that question without becoming a generic learning-and-development chapter.

The chapter moves the reader from:

> The controls exist and the required training has been completed.

To:

> **Can the people responsible for the work recognise a material problem, interpret what it means, challenge the AI appropriately and use the organisation's intervention pathways in practice?**

The chapter must preserve two boundaries:

1. capability is part of governance, but capability does not replace policy, infrastructure, authority or assurance;
2. compliance evidence such as training records remains useful, but it is not equivalent to demonstrated capability.

## Concise evidence answer

Current evidence supports the conclusion that meaningful AI-related capability is broader than tool familiarity or course completion. It includes:

- sufficient AI literacy to recognise AI participation, capabilities, limits and risks;
- domain and task expertise needed to judge the work rather than merely operate the tool;
- the ability to evaluate outputs, uncertainty, evidence and failure conditions;
- calibrated reliance — neither automatic acceptance nor reflexive rejection;
- maintained professional judgement and opportunities to practise it;
- role-specific understanding of authority, escalation, intervention and accountability;
- confidence and psychological permission to raise concerns;
- accessible channels through which challenge can produce action;
- organisational support, time, resources, feedback and learning conditions that allow capability to transfer into work.

The evidence does not support treating capability as a stable personal attribute demonstrated by one training event. Training transfer research distinguishes learning in a course from generalisation and maintenance in the workplace. Human-factors evidence shows that reliance on automation depends on task, system, user and interface conditions. Workplace AI studies show both augmentation and risk: AI assistance can improve performance, particularly for less experienced workers, while high confidence in generative AI can be associated with lower self-reported critical-thinking effort.

The defensible governing proposition is:

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

Supporting formulation:

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

These are Gardner organisational synthesis informed by established literacy, training-transfer, human-factors, expertise, workplace-learning and employee-voice research. They are not presented as externally validated definitions or a new competency framework.

## Primary and authoritative evidence

### European Union AI Act — AI literacy and oversight competence

Article 4 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 requires providers and deployers, within its scope, to take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf. The requirement takes account of technical knowledge, experience, education, training, context of use and the people affected.

Article 26(2), for defined high-risk systems, requires deployers to assign human oversight to people with the necessary competence, training, authority and support.

**Evidence contribution:** current regulation distinguishes literacy and training from the wider conditions required for effective oversight.

**Boundary:** the Act is jurisdiction- and scope-specific and does not provide a complete universal model of organisational capability.

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI RMF Core requires:

- clear roles, responsibilities and communication;
- personnel and partners trained to perform AI-risk-management responsibilities;
- differentiated roles for human-AI configurations and oversight;
- diverse disciplines, experience, expertise and backgrounds in AI-risk decisions;
- a culture that supports critical thinking and communicates AI risk.

**Evidence contribution:** capability includes role clarity, training, diversity of expertise and a critical-thinking culture rather than technical knowledge alone.

**Boundary:** the AI RMF is voluntary and context-sensitive. NIST has indicated that AI RMF 1.0 is under revision, so current wording requires a publication-stage currency check.

### Australian Government Guidance for AI Adoption

Current Australian guidance requires organisations to:

- define and communicate competencies and authority for accountable people;
- evaluate broad and role-specific AI-literacy and training needs;
- keep skills current as AI use evolves;
- train people responsible for legal obligations, personal information, operation, intervention, termination, monitoring, procurement, deployment and safe development;
- maintain operational accountability, capability and human oversight throughout the lifecycle;
- ensure users and overseers understand capabilities, limitations, failure modes and when intervention is required;
- provide accessible feedback, challenge, escalation and redress processes.

**Evidence contribution:** capability must be role-specific, current, connected to lifecycle responsibilities and supported by usable escalation and intervention mechanisms.

**Boundary:** the material is authoritative Australian guidance rather than a universal legal rule.

## Academic and workplace evidence

### AI literacy

Long and Magerko define AI literacy as competencies enabling people to critically evaluate AI, communicate and collaborate with it and use it as a tool. Ng and colleagues distinguish knowing and understanding AI, using and applying it, evaluating and creating with it, and engaging with ethical issues.

**Evidence contribution:** AI literacy is broader than prompt use or product familiarity and includes critical evaluation and ethical understanding.

**Boundary:** these frameworks are largely conceptual and educational. They do not prove that a person can exercise sound judgement in a particular workplace decision.

### Training transfer

Baldwin and Ford distinguish training inputs and learning from the generalisation and maintenance of trained knowledge and skills in the work environment. Grossman and Salas synthesise evidence showing that transfer depends on trainee characteristics, training design and workplace conditions including support, opportunity and reinforcement.

**Evidence contribution:** attendance, completion and short-term knowledge do not establish sustained operational capability.

**Boundary:** transfer factors vary across settings and tasks; no single support mechanism guarantees transfer.

### Appropriate reliance and automation bias

Human-factors research distinguishes appropriate use from misuse, disuse and abuse of automation. Lee and See argue for calibrated trust rather than maximum trust. Parasuraman and Manzey, Goddard and colleagues, and later studies show that automation bias and over-reliance are real but context-dependent.

Tomsett and colleagues emphasise uncertainty-aware information and human expertise. Zhang, Liao and Bellamy show that confidence and explanation do not consistently improve joint decision performance. Buçinca and colleagues demonstrate that cognitive-forcing interventions can reduce over-reliance, while also introducing effort and performance trade-offs.

**Evidence contribution:** capability includes knowing when to rely, when to verify and when to depart from AI advice. Explanations and confidence scores are not substitutes for judgement.

### Human-AI performance

Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone's preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis of 106 experimental studies found that human-AI combinations did not, on average, outperform the better of humans or AI alone. Outcomes varied by task type and relative human/AI capability.

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond found that a generative-AI assistant increased customer-support productivity by 15 per cent on average in a study of 5,172 agents, with larger gains for less experienced and lower-skilled workers.

**Evidence contribution:** AI can augment performance and spread aspects of expert practice, but collaboration must be designed rather than assumed to be synergistic.

**Boundary:** performance effects are context-specific. Productivity in customer support does not establish safe or effective performance in all knowledge work or consequential decisions.

### Critical thinking and cognitive effort

Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers and analysed 936 examples of generative-AI use. Higher confidence in generative AI was associated with lower self-reported critical-thinking effort. Participants described shifts from information gathering to verification, from problem-solving to response integration, and from task execution to task stewardship.

**Evidence contribution:** AI can change where cognitive effort occurs. Capability may need to move from producing first-pass work toward verification, integration and stewardship.

**Boundary:** the study is cross-sectional and self-reported. It does not establish that generative AI inevitably causes critical-thinking decline.

### Workplace training needs and worker empowerment

Zhang and colleagues used an international workshop and interviews with knowledge workers and workplace stakeholders. Participants identified needs for foundational AI understanding, critical validation, awareness of bias and worker rights, data-privacy capability, grievance mechanisms and organisational change grounded in trust and feedback.

**Evidence contribution:** responsible workplace training must connect AI understanding with domain expertise, worker rights, validation, challenge and recourse.

**Boundary:** the qualitative sample and focus on knowledge workers limit generalisation.

### Organisational AI readiness

Jöhnk, Weißert and Wyrtki identify organisational AI-readiness factors including strategic alignment, resources, knowledge, culture and data. Their work supports treating capability as an organisational condition rather than a collection of individual training records.

**Evidence contribution:** workforce knowledge operates alongside leadership, resources, culture, data and organisational routines.

**Boundary:** readiness for adoption is not the same as demonstrated capability to govern a deployed system throughout its lifecycle.

### Psychological safety, voice and challenge

Edmondson links psychological safety with team learning and interpersonal risk-taking. Detert and Burris show that leader openness affects employee voice. Morrison's review of voice and silence identifies individual, relational and organisational influences. O'Donovan and McAuliffe's systematic review finds that interventions aimed at psychological safety and speaking up are heterogeneous and context-dependent.

**Evidence contribution:** people are more likely to raise uncertainty, errors and concerns where speaking up is interpersonally and organisationally safe and leaders respond constructively.

**Boundary:** psychological safety is not sufficient. People also require relevant knowledge, attention, evidence, time, authority, escalation channels and confidence that action can follow.

## Evidence by capability domain

### 1. AI literacy and recognition

**Evidence establishes:**
- AI literacy includes recognising AI, understanding capabilities and limits, critical evaluation, effective use and ethical awareness;
- current regulation and guidance require literacy proportionate to role and context;
- technical or coding expertise is not required for every role.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
People cannot govern AI participation they do not recognise or understand sufficiently to question.

**Safe formulation:**
> AI literacy provides the minimum language and concepts needed to recognise AI participation, understand relevant limitations and engage critically with its use.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> AI literacy proves that a person is competent to oversee any AI system.

### 2. Domain and task judgement

**Evidence establishes:**
- human expertise can complement AI error patterns;
- accountable people need role-specific knowledge of the task, consequence and system limits;
- AI may support less experienced workers, but high-stakes judgement still depends on context and relevant expertise.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
A person can be AI-literate and still lack the professional or operational knowledge needed to judge a particular outcome.

**Safe formulation:**
> Meaningful oversight requires enough domain and task understanding to recognise when an output is plausible, incomplete, inappropriate or outside the intended conditions.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Only senior experts should use or oversee AI.

Capability may be distributed across teams and supported by well-designed systems, escalation and access to expertise.

### 3. Calibrated reliance

**Evidence establishes:**
- appropriate reliance differs from maximum trust;
- over-reliance and under-reliance can both reduce performance;
- reliability, uncertainty, interface design, experience and task characteristics influence reliance;
- explanations do not consistently improve calibration.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
The practical capability is not simply trusting or distrusting AI. It is knowing when to accept, verify, challenge, defer or override.

**Safe formulation:**
> Capable human-AI work depends on calibrated reliance matched to the task, system and consequence.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> More explainability automatically produces better human judgement.

### 4. Training transfer and practice

**Evidence establishes:**
- training outcomes must generalise and persist in the work environment;
- opportunity to practise, feedback, relevance, supervisor and peer support, and work design affect transfer;
- course completion is evidence of participation rather than operating competence.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
AI capability should be developed and tested in realistic work conditions, including difficult and abnormal cases.

**Safe formulation:**
> Training contributes to capability only when people can apply and maintain the relevant knowledge and judgement in their actual work.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Training programmes do not work.

Well-designed, supported and practised training can improve capability.

### 5. Expertise development and retention

**Evidence establishes:**
- automation can redistribute cognitive work and reduce opportunities to perform some tasks manually;
- AI can also expose less experienced workers to useful patterns and improve performance;
- current evidence on long-term generative-AI deskilling remains limited and context-dependent.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Organisations need to decide which forms of human judgement must be practised and retained because their accountability model depends on them.

**Safe formulation:**
> AI may augment, redistribute or weaken human expertise depending on how work, feedback and learning are designed.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> AI inevitably deskills the workforce.

### 6. Manager and accountable-role capability

**Evidence establishes:**
- official guidance requires accountable people to understand system capabilities, limits, failure modes, oversight and intervention;
- role clarity, authority, resources and training are distinct conditions;
- organisational readiness depends partly on leadership and management capability.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Managers require a capability beyond general tool use: they must understand where AI participates in work, what judgement remains human, which signals require scrutiny and whether employees are being given usable oversight responsibilities.

**Safe formulation:**
> Managers need role-specific capability to govern work allocation, challenge, escalation and intervention — not merely the ability to use the tool themselves.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Every manager must become an AI engineer.

### 7. Voice, escalation and psychological permission

**Evidence establishes:**
- psychological safety supports learning behaviour and speaking up;
- leader openness and response affect employee voice;
- accessible challenge, grievance and escalation mechanisms are recommended in current guidance;
- speaking up is influenced by interpersonal risk, hierarchy, workload, attention and perceived efficacy.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Knowledge remains inert where people do not believe challenge is legitimate, safe or likely to produce action.

**Safe formulation:**
> Capability includes the practical confidence and organisational permission to surface uncertainty, question AI-shaped work and use escalation pathways without unreasonable penalty.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> A psychologically safe culture guarantees that people will identify and report AI problems.

### 8. Evidence of capability

**Evidence establishes:**
- literacy frameworks describe competencies;
- training-transfer research requires work application and maintenance;
- oversight guidance ties competence to assigned responsibilities and intervention;
- no universal validated organisational measure of AI-governance capability currently exists.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Evidence of capability should be proportionate and role-specific, using work samples, scenario responses, observed judgement, escalation exercises, intervention drills, feedback and outcome review where appropriate.

**Safe formulation:**
> Capability should be evidenced through relevant performance in context, not inferred solely from attendance or confidence.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> One assessment can certify permanent AI-governance competence across roles and systems.

## Challenge and contrary evidence

### AI can improve performance and accelerate learning

The customer-support evidence shows substantial productivity gains, especially for less experienced workers. AI may help diffuse useful practices and reduce the time needed to perform unfamiliar work.

**Implication:** do not frame AI as inherently deskilling or human capability as valuable only when work remains manual.

### Human-AI combination is not automatically superior

The meta-analysis found no average human-AI synergy against the better of human or AI alone, with losses in many decision tasks and different outcomes in creation tasks.

**Implication:** capability development must be paired with deliberate task allocation and workflow design.

### Expertise does not eliminate bias

Experienced people can be overconfident, inattentive or biased. Domain expertise is necessary in many settings but is not a guarantee of correct judgement.

**Implication:** retain monitoring, evidence, peer challenge, independent review and system design.

### Training can work

Training-transfer research does not support dismissing formal learning. It shows that transfer depends on relevance, design, practice, support, opportunity and reinforcement.

**Implication:** the chapter should oppose training-as-proof, not training itself.

### Psychological safety is not sufficient

Employees may feel safe yet fail to notice an ambiguous threat, lack the skill to interpret it or believe speaking up will not change the outcome.

**Implication:** capability, attention, authority, channels and leader response must accompany psychological safety.

### Confidence is not competence

Confidence can be under- or over-calibrated. High confidence in AI may reduce scrutiny, while excessive distrust may cause people to reject beneficial support.

**Implication:** assess performance and judgement rather than self-confidence alone.

### Capability may be collective

One person need not possess every technical, domain, legal and operational capability. Teams and governance chains can combine complementary expertise.

**Implication:** assess the accountable arrangement, not only isolated individuals.

### Capability requirements can become exclusionary or burdensome

Overly broad competence standards can shift responsibility onto workers, create unnecessary credential barriers or demand impossible vigilance.

**Implication:** define role-specific, proportionate expectations and retain institutional responsibility for design, resources, workload and system conditions.

## Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes

- AI literacy includes understanding, use, evaluation and ethical awareness;
- training must transfer into work to affect performance;
- appropriate reliance depends on calibration, task and system conditions;
- AI can augment performance while also changing cognitive effort and expertise development;
- organisational readiness includes knowledge, resources, culture, data and leadership;
- psychological safety and leader openness support voice, but speaking up remains context-dependent;
- current regulation and guidance require role-appropriate literacy, competence, training, authority, support and challenge mechanisms.

### Reasonable interpretation

- training records are incomplete evidence of governance capability;
- capability is role-specific, situated and distributed across people and systems;
- organisations must preserve the forms of judgement on which accountability depends;
- managers need capability to govern human-AI work, not merely use AI tools;
- people must be able and permitted to question, escalate and intervene;
- capability requires continuous practice, feedback and reassessment as work and systems change.

### Gardner synthesis

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

The second formulation does not dismiss compliance. It distinguishes administrative evidence of activity from evidence that people can perform the required judgement and action.

## Claims safe to use

- AI literacy is necessary for many roles but does not by itself prove operational competence.
- Training completion does not establish that learning has transferred into work.
- Capability includes domain judgement, critical evaluation, calibrated reliance and knowledge of intervention and escalation.
- Appropriate reliance is more useful than maximum trust.
- Explanations and confidence scores do not consistently improve human-AI judgement.
- AI can augment, redistribute or weaken expertise depending on work design and use.
- Human-AI combinations do not automatically outperform the better of human or AI alone.
- Managers and accountable roles require role-specific governance capability.
- Psychological safety and leader response affect whether people raise concerns.
- Capability can be distributed across a team or governance arrangement.
- Evidence of capability should be role-specific, proportionate and connected to performance in context.

## Claims requiring qualification

- Do not claim that AI inevitably deskills workers.
- Do not claim that literacy or training automatically improves decisions.
- Do not generalise customer-support productivity effects to all work.
- Do not treat self-reported critical-thinking effort as causal evidence of cognitive decline.
- Do not imply that more human involvement is automatically safer.
- Do not treat psychological safety as sufficient for voice or problem detection.
- Do not imply that every individual must possess every specialised capability.
- Do not present a role-specific exercise or assessment as permanent certification.
- Apply EU AI Act requirements only within their legal scope.

## Narrative opportunity

### Opening tension

Use a composite organisation with a high AI-training completion rate and a green governance dashboard. During a realistic tabletop exercise, a team receives a plausible AI-supported recommendation that contains a material but non-obvious weakness.

The infrastructure works as designed:

- the relevant limitation is documented;
- an uncertainty signal is available;
- an escalation route exists;
- a manager has authority to pause the workflow.

Yet the team does not intervene.

One employee recognises that something is wrong but assumes the system has been approved and remains silent. The manager has completed the required training but cannot explain the significance of the signal or identify the escalation threshold. Another person treats the fluent recommendation as corroboration rather than as an output requiring judgement.

The dashboard can prove that training occurred. The exercise cannot prove that capability exists.

Opening question:

> **What exactly did the organisation believe the training record proved?**

### Reader discovery

The reader should discover that capability is not a possession stored in a learning-management system. It is a working relationship among knowledge, judgement, practice, task design, authority, evidence, voice and organisational response.

### Dominant proposition

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

### Ending movement

The chapter should end after establishing that capable people still need governance that gives them legitimate room to act. Training someone to exercise judgement is futile where policy, approvals, incentives or hierarchy make the exercise of that judgement excessively slow, risky or procedurally impossible.

This leads directly into Chapter 8:

> **Does the governance system give capable people legitimate room to act?**
