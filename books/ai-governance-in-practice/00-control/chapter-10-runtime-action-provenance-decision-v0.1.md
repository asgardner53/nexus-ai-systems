## Approved proposition package

> **When AI can act, governance must be enforceable at the point where authority becomes action.**

> **Delegated action should remain bounded, attributable, observable, interruptible and revocable, with reversibility or defined recourse proportionate to consequence.**

> **The relevant governance object is the complete action path connecting identity, authority, tools, state changes, counterparties, evidence and recourse — not the model or final output alone.**

These formulations are approved as **Gardner book-level synthesis** informed by established identity, security, human-control, provenance, assurance and agent-governance evidence. They are not approved as legal rules, technical standards or externally validated definitions.

The phrase *action path* remains descriptive lower-case wording. It is not a new named construct.

## Claims permitted

The manuscript may state that:

- governance significance lies in what a system can cause to happen, not merely the product label;
- identity and technical capability do not establish permission for a particular action;
- material permissions should be scoped, time-limited where appropriate and evaluated in context;
- action-level controls, sandboxing and restricted tools can reduce consequences;
- high-impact or difficult-to-reverse actions may require stronger prior approval;
- human approval is weak where reviewers lack time, evidence, authority or a real alternative;

- multi-agent arrangements require system-level governance and evaluation;
- permission and evaluation should be revisited after material change;
- organisations remain accountable for the authority they delegate and the operating arrangements they create.

## Design boundaries

### Agentic is a spectrum

Govern the actual combination of planning, tool use, action, persistence, delegation, learning and supervision. Do not make the chapter depend on a disputed label.

### Identity is not authority

A verified principal may still lack permission for the requested action, resource, amount, counterpart, time or purpose.

### Runtime enforcement is not total automation of governance

Some boundaries can be machine enforced. Others require professional, human and institutional judgement.

### Intervention is plural

Use refusal, approval, pause, cancellation, revocation, rollback, fallback, containment, safe degradation, decommissioning and recourse as appropriate. Do not promise one universal stop mechanism.

### Traceability remains proportionate

Retain what is needed for intervention and material governance claims without collecting every possible trace indefinitely.

### Multi-agent governance follows practical reach

Distinguish what the organisation controls, influences, depends upon and accepts as residual risk across singular, federated and open arrangements.

### Accountability remains institutional

An agent can execute delegated authority. It cannot absorb responsibility for purpose, boundary, control, evidence, remedy or withdrawal.

## Hostile-evidence controls retained

The manuscript must preserve that:

- many agentic risks are evolutionary extensions of established automation, identity and cybersecurity problems;
- bounded delegated action can create legitimate value;
- approval fatigue can make human review ceremonial;
- runtime controls may fail or enforce a poor boundary;
- action traces may be incomplete or create privacy and security risk;
- abrupt shutdown may create unsafe partial state;
- protocol compliance does not equal institutional governance;
- safe components may combine into an unsafe arrangement;
- benchmark and evaluation results remain context-bound.

## Approved chapter role

Chapter 10 will begin with the knowledge assistant receiving one bounded action permission and then interacting with another agent and downstream workflow in a way that expands consequence without one explicit decision to authorise the complete path.

The chapter will integrate:

- agentic AI as a spectrum;
- identity and action-level authorisation;
- tools, credentials and state changes;
- runtime enforcement and containment;
- selective human approval and meaningful intervention;
- revocation, rollback, fallback and recourse;
- action evidence and oversight limits;
- multi-agent and cross-organisational governance reach;
- adaptive reassessment after material change;
- institutional accountability.

The chapter will close the book by returning to its central question: can the organisation still exercise judgement, assign authority, intervene and remain accountable for what it permits AI to do?
