## Evidence conclusion

The evidence supports five practical conditions for meaningful accountability in AI-mediated work:

1. capability;
2. visibility;
3. authority;
4. intervention capacity;
5. accountability alignment.

The evidence does not support presenting these component ideas as newly discovered, as the first role-level test, as a validated measurement instrument, or as a universal legal standard.

The defensible Gardner contribution is the concise organisational diagnostic, its linkage to the Accountability–Control Gap, its application to people, roles and organisational arrangements, and its integration into the book's governance operating system.

## Claim register

### C04-001 — Formal human presence or sign-off is insufficient for meaningful accountability

- **Claim:** Human presence, final approval or a nominal override right does not by itself establish that accountability can be exercised meaningfully.
- **Claim type:** Verified fact / supported interpretation
- **Intended use:** Chapter 4 governing problem
- **Primary evidence:** EU AI Act Articles 14 and 26(2); NIST AI RMF Govern; Sterz et al. (2024); Zhu et al. (2026)
- **Supporting evidence:** Green (2022); Laux (2023); Siebert et al. (2022/2023); Australian Guidance for AI Adoption
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Human review can improve outcomes in suitable settings; the problem is not human involvement itself but unsupported, symbolic or poorly designed involvement.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct for general governance scholarship; EU legal requirements are jurisdiction-specific
- **Verification status:** Verified
- **Safe wording:** Human presence or sign-off is not sufficient evidence that the accountable person can understand, challenge or change an AI-mediated decision.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Human review is ineffective or unnecessary.
- **Notes/limitations:** The appropriate form of human involvement varies by context, consequence, autonomy and reversibility.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — author wording and doctrine boundary

### C04-002 — Capability is a condition of meaningful accountability

- **Claim:** The accountable arrangement must contain enough task, domain and system understanding to exercise independent judgement.
- **Claim type:** Verified fact / supported interpretation
- **Intended use:** Meaningful Accountability Test — Capability
- **Primary evidence:** Siebert et al. (2022/2023); Sterz et al. (2024); EU AI Act Article 26(2); Australian Guidance for AI Adoption
- **Supporting evidence:** Flemisch et al. (2011); Zhu et al. (2026); Nitchals (2026); van de Sande et al. (2026)
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Full technical model comprehension is not required in every setting; expertise may be distributed across a team or governance chain.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct as an organisational principle; specific competency requirements remain context-dependent
- **Verification status:** Verified
- **Safe wording:** The accountable person, role or team must understand the task, the system's role, relevant risks and known limits well enough to exercise independent judgement.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Every accountable manager must understand the model's technical internals.
- **Notes/limitations:** Training records are evidence of activity, not proof that judgement remains usable.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — framework wording

### C04-003 — Visibility is a condition of meaningful accountability

- **Claim:** Accountability requires timely, usable access to the evidence and signals needed to recognise when scrutiny, challenge or escalation is required.
- **Claim type:** Verified fact / supported interpretation
- **Intended use:** Meaningful Accountability Test — Visibility
- **Primary evidence:** Sterz et al. (2024) on epistemic access; EU AI Act Article 14; NIST AI RMF; Zhu et al. (2026)
- **Supporting evidence:** Tomsett et al. (2020); Lee & See (2004); Nitchals (2026); van de Sande et al. (2026)
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Transparency artefacts, explanations and confidence scores do not automatically produce understanding or better decisions.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct with task-specific qualification
- **Verification status:** Verified
- **Safe wording:** Visibility means decision-relevant information that is sufficiently timely, usable and intelligible for the accountable role to recognise when action is required.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Full technical transparency guarantees meaningful oversight.
- **Notes/limitations:** Visibility and capability are separate: information may be available without being understood, and expertise may exist without current evidence.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — framework wording

### C04-004 — Authority is a condition of meaningful accountability

- **Claim:** The accountable role must possess recognised and protected authority to question, defer, override, escalate or stop the relevant action.
- **Claim type:** Verified fact / supported interpretation
- **Intended use:** Meaningful Accountability Test — Authority
- **Primary evidence:** Flemisch et al. (2011); Siebert et al. (2022/2023); EU AI Act Articles 14 and 26(2); NIST AI RMF Govern
- **Supporting evidence:** OECD AI Principles; Australian Guidance for AI Adoption; Laux (2023); Nitchals (2026)
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** A written authority may be undermined by incentives, management pressure, defaults, workload or sanctions for disagreement.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct as governance principle; legal authority varies by jurisdiction and role
- **Verification status:** Verified
- **Safe wording:** Authority must exist in practice, not merely in a role description or policy.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** A formal override right proves that meaningful authority exists.
- **Notes/limitations:** Capability does not confer authority. A person may know what should happen while lacking permission or standing to make it happen.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — use of Gardner shorthand

### C04-005 — Intervention capacity is distinct from authority

- **Claim:** A person may possess legitimate authority yet lack the time, mechanism, resources, support or reversibility needed to alter the outcome.
- **Claim type:** Verified fact / supported interpretation
- **Intended use:** Meaningful Accountability Test — Intervention capacity
- **Primary evidence:** Sterz et al. (2024) on causal power and practical inhibitors; EU AI Act Article 14; Australian Guidance for AI Adoption
- **Supporting evidence:** Nitchals (2026) on temporal capacity and intervention power; van de Sande et al. (2026) on intervention effectiveness; Green (2022); NIST AI RMF
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Not every use requires real-time human intervention; some controls operate prospectively, collectively or through system-level governance.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct with use-context qualification
- **Verification status:** Verified
- **Safe wording:** The accountable arrangement must be able to act in time, through usable means and with sufficient organisational support to change the trajectory before avoidable harm becomes irreversible.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** A stop button is sufficient to establish meaningful control.
- **Notes/limitations:** Intervention may occur at output, workflow, configuration, escalation or system level depending on the use.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — framework wording

### C04-006 — Accountability should be aligned with practical control

- **Claim:** Responsibility should be proportionate to the ability, authority and practical control a person, role or organisation can realistically exercise.
- **Claim type:** Verified interpretation / Gardner synthesis
- **Intended use:** Meaningful Accountability Test — Accountability alignment
- **Primary evidence:** Flemisch et al. (2011); Siebert et al. (2022/2023); OECD AI Principles
- **Supporting evidence:** Elish (2019); Sterz et al. (2024); NIST AI RMF; Nitchals (2026); van de Sande et al. (2026)
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Organisational accountability can remain broader than direct operational control; accountability is often distributed and may include responsibility for system design, delegation and governance choices.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct as governance interpretation; not a universal liability rule
- **Verification status:** Verified with qualification
- **Safe wording:** Responsibility should be proportionate to the control that can realistically be exercised, while distributed organisational responsibility remains visible and legible.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** A person cannot be accountable for anything they do not directly control.
- **Notes/limitations:** Accountability alignment does not eliminate executive or institutional responsibility for delegation and system design.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — high-materiality Gardner synthesis

### C04-007 — The Meaningful Accountability Test is a Gardner organisational diagnostic

- **Claim:** The five-condition test can be used as a practical diagnostic to examine whether assigned accountability is usable in an AI-mediated decision arrangement.
- **Claim type:** Gardner synthesis / emerging doctrine
- **Intended use:** Central Chapter 4 framework
- **Primary evidence:** Synthesises C04-001 to C04-006
- **Supporting evidence:** Flemisch et al.; Siebert et al.; Sterz et al.; Zhu et al.; NIST; OECD; EU AI Act; Australian guidance
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Material prior art presents similar four- and seven-condition models; the test has not been empirically validated as a scale or predictor.
- **Source quality:** A for component evidence; synthesis is author-controlled
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Cross-sector diagnostic, requiring contextual interpretation
- **Verification status:** Supported for use as Gardner synthesis
- **Safe wording:** The Meaningful Accountability Test is a practical organisational diagnostic that brings established evidence into a concise set of questions about capability, visibility, authority, intervention capacity and accountability alignment.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** The test is the first framework of its kind, a validated instrument, a universal legal standard or a definitive compliance test.
- **Notes/limitations:** Its contribution lies in organisational packaging, linkage to the Accountability–Control Gap and integration into the book's governance operating system.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — Alec Gardner final approval

- **Primary evidence:** Nitchals (2026) bottleneck reasoning; Sterz et al. (2024) necessary conditions; Siebert et al. (2022/2023) commensurateness
- **Supporting evidence:** EU AI Act combined oversight requirements; van de Sande et al. (2026)
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** No universal threshold or empirically validated non-compensability rule exists across all sectors and uses.
- **Source quality:** B+
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Indirect-to-direct depending on context
- **Verification status:** Provisionally supported
- **Safe wording:** A material weakness in one condition may make accountability fragile even where other conditions appear strong; the significance depends on context and consequence.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Failure of any single item automatically makes every AI use unlawful or ungovernable.
- **Notes/limitations:** Use structured judgement rather than mechanical scoring.
- **Human approval required:** Yes

### C04-009 — Passing the diagnostic does not itself justify deployment

- **Claim:** Even where the five conditions appear adequate, the system may remain inappropriate, unnecessary or insufficiently safe to deploy.
- **Claim type:** Supported interpretation / challenge control
- **Intended use:** Prevent the framework becoming an approval checklist
- **Primary evidence:** Green (2022); NIST context and go/no-go logic; Australian risk screening and proportionality guidance
- **Supporting evidence:** Laux (2023); human-factors literature
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** Effective oversight can be one legitimate control among others where the underlying use is otherwise justified.
- **Source quality:** A-
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct as governance principle
- **Verification status:** Verified as qualified interpretation
- **Safe wording:** The test examines whether accountability is usable; it does not determine that the AI use should proceed.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Passing the five conditions proves the system is safe, ethical, compliant or suitable.
- **Notes/limitations:** A no-go decision remains available.
- **Human approval required:** No additional gate beyond framework approval

### C04-010 — Capability does not confer authority

- **Claim:** A person may possess the knowledge and skill to judge an AI-mediated decision while lacking the legitimate organisational authority to change it.
- **Claim type:** Supported interpretation / Gardner shorthand
- **Intended use:** Chapter 4 distinction between Capability and Authority

- **Supporting evidence:** NIST AI RMF Govern; Australian Guidance for AI Adoption
- **Contrary/limiting evidence:** In some roles capability and authority are intentionally combined, but they remain analytically distinct.
- **Source quality:** A
- **Currency check:** 2026-11-18
- **Jurisdiction/population fit:** Direct as organisational interpretation
- **Verification status:** Supported
- **Safe wording:** Capability does not confer authority; organisations must deliberately assign both.
- **Unsafe overstatement:** Expertise always entitles a person to make the final decision.
- **Notes/limitations:** Gardner shorthand, not a quoted standard or universal legal rule.
- **Human approval required:** Yes — already approved as emerging manuscript proposition

## Contradiction log

### CON-04-001 — Human involvement may worsen decisions

- **Claim affected:** C04-001 to C04-005
- **Evidence A:** Oversight can mitigate risk when conditions are adequate.
- **Evidence B:** Automation-bias and algorithm-in-the-loop research shows humans may accept incorrect advice, perform inconsistently or add little independent value.
- **Nature of conflict:** Human presence is neither inherently protective nor inherently harmful.
- **Resolution:** Assess the quality and consequence of human agency rather than maximising human touch.
- **Changes safe wording:** Yes
- **Status:** Resolved in framework boundary

### CON-04-002 — Automation does not always erode capability

- **Claim affected:** C04-002
- **Evidence A:** Out-of-the-loop, deskilling and situation-awareness research identifies degradation risks.
- **Evidence B:** Some restricted and predictable industrial settings do not show perceived deskilling as a material problem.
- **Nature of conflict:** Effect depends on task, reliability, environment, time pressure and retained practice.
- **Resolution:** Use 'can' and context qualification; do not claim inevitable erosion.
- **Status:** Resolved

### CON-04-003 — More information does not necessarily improve judgement

- **Claim affected:** C04-003
- **Evidence A:** Epistemic access and interpretable information support oversight.
- **Evidence B:** Explanations and confidence information do not consistently improve decisions; they may increase cognitive load or misplaced reliance.
- **Nature of conflict:** Information quantity is not the same as decision-relevant visibility.
- **Resolution:** Define visibility as timely, usable and intelligible evidence matched to the task.
- **Status:** Resolved

### CON-04-004 — Accountability may exceed direct operational control

- **Claim affected:** C04-006
- **Evidence A:** Responsibility should be commensurate with ability, authority and control.
- **Evidence B:** Executives and institutions may remain accountable for choices to delegate, procure, design and govern systems even without direct case-level control.
- **Nature of conflict:** Direct-control alignment versus broader institutional responsibility.
- **Resolution:** Align role-specific responsibility with practical control while preserving organisational accountability for delegation and system design.
- **Status:** Resolved

### CON-04-005 — Oversight adequacy does not equal deployment suitability

- **Claim affected:** C04-009
- **Evidence A:** Effective oversight is a legitimate risk-control mechanism.
- **Evidence B:** Some systems should not be deployed; human oversight can create false comfort.
- **Nature of conflict:** Control effectiveness versus underlying use justification.
- **Resolution:** Treat the test as one governance diagnostic, not an approval certificate.
- **Status:** Resolved
