# Chapter 2 — When Policy Exists but Control Does Not

By the time many organisations begin to take AI governance seriously, they already have the beginnings of a respectable governance architecture. There is a policy. There are principles. Someone has created an approval form. A committee has been given oversight. A register may list approved systems and use cases. Training has been rolled out, perhaps unevenly, and senior leaders can point to a document that says the organisation is committed to responsible AI.

From a distance, the picture can look reassuring because the visible elements of governance are present. The difficulty appears when the organisation is asked to move from declared intent to a live decision. Who can approve this particular use? What evidence must they see before doing so? If the system changes after approval, who notices? If the risk increases, who has authority to stop it? What happens when the business owner wants to proceed but the risk function is uncomfortable? Who monitors whether the control designed six months earlier still works?

These questions expose a difference that is easy to miss when governance is discussed mainly through policies and frameworks. It is one thing to state what the organisation believes should happen. It is another to create the organisational conditions that make those intentions real.

Policy matters because organisations need to make their intentions explicit. They need to state what they value, what they will not accept, what responsibilities people carry and what standards should guide judgement. The problem begins when the existence of those statements is mistaken for evidence that governance has been enacted. Organisational researchers have long examined this gap between formal arrangements and operational practice. In the management literature, the phenomenon is commonly described as **decoupling**: formal policies or structures are adopted while day-to-day practice remains only loosely connected to them (Westphal & Zajac, 2001).

The gap does not necessarily imply bad faith. Formal and operational arrangements can drift apart because incentives point in different directions, because responsibilities are unclear, because the process is too cumbersome, because the people expected to apply it lack capability, because systems were never changed to support the new requirement, or because an approval mechanism was designed far away from the work it is meant to govern. More recent regulatory scholarship makes a similar distinction between “work as imagined” in policy and “work as done” in practice (de Bree & Stoopendaal, 2020).

This matters because most organisations do not operate as neat diagrams. They operate through habits, workarounds, inherited systems, competing accountabilities and decisions made under time pressure. A policy may assume that a manager will escalate a use case when a threshold is reached, while the manager may not know that the threshold exists. A committee may believe it has oversight, while operational teams see it as advisory. A risk process may require evidence that nobody has been tasked to collect. A control may technically exist but be bypassed because the workflow makes compliance impractical.

AI does not invent this organisational problem, but it can make the consequences harder to ignore. A policy may state that human oversight is required, while the workflow gives no clear indication of when intervention is necessary. A principle may require fairness, yet the approval process may never specify what evidence of fairness is sufficient. A committee may formally own governance at the organisational level while managers, system owners and frontline teams remain uncertain about who can make the operational decision in front of them.

That is the point where policy can begin to look stronger than the control it actually creates.

The distinction is already embedded in the major governance frameworks. ISO/IEC 42001 does not describe an AI management system as a policy document. It describes a system that establishes policies and objectives **and the processes required to achieve them**, supported by implementation, performance evaluation and continual improvement. NIST takes the same view from a risk-management perspective. Its AI Risk Management Framework expects policies, processes, procedures and practices to be implemented effectively, supported by accountability structures, defined responsibilities, training, monitoring and periodic review (NIST, 2023).

The shift is subtle but important. Governance is no longer something the organisation possesses. It is something the organisation must be able to do.

That becomes clearer when governance is tested against an actual use. Imagine a business team proposes using a generative AI system to screen customer complaints and recommend which cases should be escalated. The organisation's policy may say that AI must be fair, transparent and subject to appropriate human oversight. Those principles are sensible, but they do not yet determine what happens next. Someone still has to decide what evidence would demonstrate that the screening process is sufficiently fair, what information a reviewer needs to understand why a case was prioritised, when specialist approval is required, who monitors performance after deployment and whether a change from recommendation to automatic action invalidates the original approval.

In that moment, the organisation discovers whether its policy is connected to an operating system of governance or merely sitting beside one.

The mechanisms that create that connection are often ordinary. An approval threshold determines when a use needs specialist review. A system permission prevents an AI agent from taking an action outside its authorised scope. A deployment process requires specific evidence before release. A named role has authority to pause a system when operating conditions change. Monitoring reveals that a control which once appeared adequate is no longer sufficient. None of these mechanisms is particularly glamorous, but they are the points at which principles begin to alter organisational behaviour.

This distinction becomes even clearer when we separate **instruction** from **enforceable control**. An instruction tells someone what they should do. A control changes what they can do, what they must do, or what evidence they must provide before an action can proceed. An instruction may say, “Do not use AI with confidential information.” An enforceable control may restrict access, prevent data transfer, require an approved environment, create an audit trail or block a transaction until specific conditions are met.

The distinction is not absolute. Organisations cannot technically enforce every requirement, nor should they try. Some controls depend on professional judgement, trust and escalation. Others are embedded in systems and workflows. The practical question is whether the organisation has consciously decided which behaviours rely on judgement and which require stronger boundaries.

That is one reason governance artefacts and governance capability should not be treated as the same thing. A policy, register, committee, standard, risk assessment and training module are artefacts. They are visible, reviewable and often necessary. Governance capability is the organisation's ability to use those artefacts to make, constrain, explain and revisit real decisions.

The distinction matters at executive and board level because artefacts are easy to count. A board can be told that the organisation has adopted an AI policy, created a governance committee, completed training and established a register. All of those statements may be accurate. Yet they do not reveal whether the organisation can answer the harder operational questions when a decision becomes consequential.

That gap creates a particular form of risk: **false assurance**. Leaders may believe control exists because the governance architecture is visible, while the actual links between policy, authority, execution and evidence remain weak. The problem is not deception. It is the natural tendency to treat documented structure as evidence of operational effectiveness.

The distinction between the two can be uncomfortable. A policy may say that certain uses require human approval, but the system may not record whether approval occurred. A committee may review high-risk applications, but the organisation may have no reliable way of knowing whether all high-risk uses have been identified. A procedure may require monitoring, but nobody may own the monitoring data. A manager may formally remain accountable while the decision process has become so technically complex that meaningful challenge is difficult.

At that point, the governance system is not absent. It is incomplete.

This is where the operating-system idea becomes useful. Policy sits close to **intent**: it tells the organisation what it seeks to achieve and what it considers acceptable. To become operational, that intent has to connect to **authority**: who may decide, approve, escalate or stop. Authority then needs **boundaries**: what is permitted, under what conditions, and where limits apply. Those boundaries have to shape **execution**: what people and systems actually do. Execution then has to leave **evidence** that the organisation can inspect.

That sequence is not a rigid lifecycle, and it is not the whole governance model. But it helps expose where control can disappear. If intent is clear but authority is vague, policy remains aspirational. If authority is assigned but boundaries are weak, decisions become inconsistent. If boundaries exist but are not reflected in execution, the organisation relies on memory and goodwill. If execution occurs without evidence, assurance becomes guesswork.

These are organisational design problems, not simply compliance problems.

That distinction also explains why forms and committees can be either useful or hollow depending on how they are connected to work. A committee can bring together legal, risk, technology, data, security, HR and business perspectives that no single function holds alone. An approval form can force a team to articulate purpose, risk and evidence. A register can improve visibility. But each of these tools depends on the quality of the decisions around it. A committee that has no clear decision rights may add discussion without adding control. A form that asks whether a system is “high risk” without defining the basis for that judgement may simply move uncertainty into a checkbox. A register that is not linked to approval, monitoring or review may become a catalogue rather than a control.

Current responsible-AI research shows that this translation problem is not merely theoretical. Studies of developers and governance practitioners continue to report difficulties in converting high-level commitments into usable processes, assigning responsibility across functions and adapting governance as systems and contexts change (Papagiannidis et al., 2025; Sayan & Bueechl, 2025). The 2026 Stanford AI Index likewise reports that formal responsible-AI policies are becoming more common while organisations continue to face implementation barriers involving knowledge, budget and regulatory uncertainty (Stanford HAI, 2026).

Those findings need to be read carefully. They do not show that formal governance is ineffective. In fact, the evidence points the other way when policy, standards, training and clearly assigned responsibilities are integrated into day-to-day practice. The issue is not whether organisations formalise governance. It is whether the formal elements are internalised strongly enough to influence what people and systems actually do.

This is where the temptation to use the phrase *governance theatre* needs restraint. There are certainly organisations that display policies, principles or committees in ways that create an appearance of control without materially changing practice. But weak implementation is not always theatre. Sometimes the organisation is genuinely trying to govern responsibly and has simply not solved the harder problems of authority, coordination, capability, evidence and feedback.

Those harder problems are more difficult because they require changes to the organisation itself. It is relatively easy to approve a policy. It is much harder to redesign a workflow so that the right person sees the right evidence before a decision is made. It is harder to decide which risks can be accepted locally and which require escalation. Harder again is building monitoring that can detect when the assumptions behind an approval have changed. Governance becomes demanding at precisely the point where it stops being a document and starts becoming a relationship between decisions, people, systems and controls.

Monitoring is part of that relationship because approval is never the end of the story. A model may change. A vendor may update a feature. The data may shift. The organisation may start using the system for a different purpose. What began as a low-consequence use may become more consequential as employees rely on it more heavily or as the system gains the ability to initiate actions. The original policy may remain unchanged while the risk around the use has changed substantially.

The board-level consequence is important. A governance system that cannot detect change may continue reporting that a use is “approved” long after the conditions supporting that approval have moved. The status remains green because the record has not changed, not because the risk has remained stable. In that situation, governance can produce reassurance while the underlying control environment deteriorates.

That is why continual review appears so consistently in serious governance frameworks. Governance has to remember not only what was approved, but why, under what conditions, with what evidence and with what limits. It also has to know what has changed since the approval was granted. Otherwise the organisation can become increasingly confident in its own paperwork while gradually losing sight of the system it thought it had governed.

The policy–practice gap is therefore better understood as a problem of **coupling**. Policy is one component of the governance system. Roles are another. So are permissions, controls, evidence requirements, escalation, monitoring and review. Governance becomes credible when those components reinforce one another closely enough that the organisation's stated principles survive contact with real work.

This is the point at which the distinction becomes useful: **policy tells the organisation what it intends; governance determines what actually happens.** The statement is not a dismissal of policy. It is a reminder that principles acquire organisational force only when they alter decisions, permissions, controls, evidence and behaviour.

For leaders, that changes the diagnostic question. Instead of asking only, “Do we have an AI policy?”, the more useful question becomes, “What changes in a real decision because this policy exists?” If the answer is unclear, the organisation may have declared governance without yet building the operating capability to sustain it.

That distinction becomes especially important when responsibility is assigned to a human being. A policy may state that a manager remains accountable for the final decision, and the organisation may point to that sentence as evidence that human oversight has been preserved. Yet the formal assignment of accountability does not tell us whether the person still has the practical ability to understand, challenge or control the decision.

That is the question waiting underneath the policy.

What if the human remains accountable while the practical control has already begun to move somewhere else?
