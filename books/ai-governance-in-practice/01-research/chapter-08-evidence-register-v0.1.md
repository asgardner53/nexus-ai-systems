## Research question

Under what conditions can AI governance create legitimate room for people and systems to act, rather than merely add friction, and how should organisations distinguish enabling governance from weak control, coercive formalisation, red tape or permission to ignore risk?

## Overall evidence decision

The evidence supports the chapter's central argument that formal governance can be designed to enable responsible action as well as constrain it. Enabling qualities arise through clear decision rights, useful information, repair and exception mechanisms, proportionate pathways, relevant expertise, feedback and the ability to change course. They are not inferred from the quantity of policy or process.

The evidence also establishes strong limits. Governance may become coercive, burdensome or ceremonial; risk classifications may be manipulated; exceptions can normalise erosion; clarity can harden the wrong boundary; and controls designed as enabling may be used hierarchically for surveillance or blame.

The controlled Gardner synthesis is:

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act by clarifying what may proceed, within which boundaries, under whose authority, with what evidence and when escalation is required.**

Supporting propositions:

> **The opposite of weak governance is not necessarily more governance. It is better-designed governance.**

> **Permission to act is not permission to ignore risk.**

## Safe wording and prohibited overstatement

### C08-001 — Enabling and coercive governance

**Safe:**

> Formal governance may support responsible work where it helps people understand the boundary, repair problems, coordinate decisions and adapt appropriately to varied conditions.

**Unsafe:**

> Formalisation is either enabling or coercive as an inherent and permanent property.

### C08-005 — Risk appetite

**Safe:**

> Risk appetite becomes useful when translated into decision boundaries, thresholds, authority, evidence and escalation conditions.

**Unsafe:**

> Risk appetite authorises management to accept any risk it considers commercially worthwhile.

### C08-006 — Proportionality

**Safe:**

> Proportionate governance varies the depth, independence, evidence and approval required according to the use, uncertainty and potential consequence.

**Unsafe:**

> Proportionality means reducing controls until implementation becomes convenient.

### C08-008 — Conditional approval

**Safe:**

> Conditional approval can be stronger than indefinite delay or unqualified approval where the conditions, limits, monitoring and review triggers are explicit.

**Unsafe:**

> Conditional approval proves the residual risk is acceptable.

### C08-009 — Exceptions

**Safe:**

> An exception can form part of control where it has a defined reason, authority, scope, duration, evidence and review path.

**Unsafe:**

> An exception is legitimate because it has been documented.

### C08-011 — Governance burden

**Safe:**

> Governance requirements should be tested for purpose, duplication, cumulative burden and whether they continue to change the decision they were designed to govern.

**Unsafe:**

> Any control that slows work is red tape.

### C08-016 — Governance as Permission

**Safe:**

> Governance as Permission is an organisational lens for designing governance so that people and systems can act within explicit, proportionate and reviewable boundaries, with defined authority, evidence requirements, exception paths, escalation and the ability to restrict or withdraw permission when conditions change.

**Unsafe:**

> Governance as Permission is a new universal theory proving that governance makes organisations faster, more innovative or more trusted.

## Contrary and limiting evidence

### Design intention does not control experience

A transparent or participative control can later be used for blame, surveillance or rigid comparison. The chapter must judge governance by its operation as well as its stated design.

### Clarity may preserve a poor decision

A clear boundary may still be unlawful, exclusionary, strategically weak or obsolete. Enabling governance requires review, challenge and responsiveness.

### Proportionality can be gamed

Project teams may minimise uncertainty or consequence to enter a lighter pathway. Higher-materiality classifications need evidence and independent challenge where warranted.

### Exceptions can normalise drift

Repeated exceptions may reveal a defective rule or produce cumulative control erosion. Pattern-level monitoring is necessary.

### Absence of process can reduce action

Where authority and boundaries are unclear, people may seek unnecessary approval, delay action or make inconsistent decisions. Less process is not automatically empowerment.

### Burden can undermine transparency

Excessive or duplicative controls may push activity into informal channels and divert attention from material risks.

### Responsible innovation has institutional limits

Anticipation and participation cannot overcome every power imbalance, commercial incentive, resource constraint or value conflict.

### High-reliability practices are context-dependent

The logic of attention, resilience and relevant expertise is useful, but specialised sector practices should not be imposed on every low-consequence AI use.

## Source quality and selection

### Core sources retained

- Adler & Borys (1996) — foundational enabling/coercive formalisation.
- Ahrens & Chapman (2004); Wouters & Wilderom (2008); Chapman & Kihn (2009) — enabling controls, flexibility and information.
- Cäker & Siverbo (2021) and related 2023 evidence — hierarchy and contextual loss of enabling qualities.
- van Loon et al. (2016); George et al. (2021); Bozeman et al. (2020) — red tape, burden and workarounds.
- Stilgoe et al. (2013); de Hoop et al. (2016) — responsible innovation and its limits.
- Hales & Chakravorty (2016); Etchegaray et al. (2019) — high-reliability organising.
- NIST AI RMF Playbook; Australian Government Guidance for AI Adoption; OECD AI Principles and accountability guidance.
- MIT CISR / Weill and Ross — neighbouring decision-rights governance theory.

### Excluded from carrying core claims

- very recent agentic-governance preprints with limited validation;
- specialised cybersecurity, finance, energy and DAO frameworks;
- broad 2026 ethics and organisational-governance reviews that duplicate stronger evidence;
- sector-specific 2026 maturity studies where authoritative guidance is stronger.

## Originality and provenance decision

The component ideas are established. The book does not claim originality for enabling formalisation, proportionality, decision rights, conditional approval, exception handling, high reliability, responsible innovation or red-tape research.

The approved Gardner contribution is:

1. applying enabling-governance theory directly to AI-enabled organisational work;
2. defining governance as a system that can create legitimate operating room rather than only constraint;
3. integrating permission with Meaningful Accountability, the Delegated Authority Map, governance infrastructure and Capability as Governance;
4. distinguishing permission to act from permission to ignore risk;
5. connecting proportionality, conditional approval, exceptions, escalation and revocation in one executive-facing lens;
6. creating the bridge to Chapter 9, where permission must leave evidence capable of reconstruction and assurance.
