## Research question

Under what conditions can AI governance create legitimate room for people and systems to act, rather than merely add friction, and how should organisations distinguish enabling governance from weak control, coercive formalisation, red tape or permission to ignore risk?

## Chapter role

Chapter 7 established that capable people cannot exercise meaningful judgement where the organisation has not preserved the knowledge, practice, authority, confidence and escalation conditions needed to act.

It ended with the question:

> **Does the governance system give capable people legitimate room to act?**

Chapter 8 must answer that question without becoming an argument for less governance.

The chapter moves the reader from:

> Governance protects the organisation by requiring more review, more approval and more control.

To:

> **Governance is effective when it makes responsible action possible within clear, proportionate and reviewable boundaries.**

The chapter must preserve three boundaries:

1. permission is bounded organisational authority, not the absence of control;
2. enabling governance does not mean that every use should proceed;
3. governance may support speed, confidence or innovation in some settings, but the evidence does not justify treating those outcomes as automatic.

## Concise evidence answer

The evidence supports a distinction between formalisation that helps people understand and perform work and formalisation that mainly compels compliance, centralises control or adds burden without improving the rule's purpose.

Foundational work on enabling versus coercive bureaucracy shows that formal systems can support task mastery where they help users understand how the process works, repair problems, see how their work connects to the wider system and adapt appropriately to varied conditions. Later field studies show that control systems can support flexibility, learning and performance when they are developed with users, supply useful information and permit repair and adjustment. The same controls can become coercive where information is used mainly for surveillance, exceptions are treated as failure, hierarchical accountability dominates learning or local judgement has no legitimate place.

Current AI-risk frameworks reach a compatible practical conclusion. NIST, OECD and Australian Government guidance call for governance proportionate to context, risk, autonomy and consequence. They recognise risk tolerance, conditional approval, go/no-go decisions, differentiated oversight, escalation, course correction, restriction and decommissioning. They do not prescribe identical controls for every use.

The evidence also establishes a serious counterweight. Excessive, poorly designed or functionally weak rules can create red tape, administrative burden, workarounds and non-compliance. Responsible-innovation processes can become ceremonial, exclude affected people or fail where authority, resources and incentives do not support responsiveness. High-reliability organising shows that disciplined operations require shared baselines and attention to weak signals, while also preserving deference to relevant expertise and adaptation when the unexpected occurs.

The defensible Gardner synthesis is therefore:

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act by clarifying what may proceed, within which boundaries, under whose authority, with what evidence and when escalation is required.**

Supporting proposition:

> **The opposite of weak governance is not necessarily more governance. It is better-designed governance.**

These formulations are an organisational synthesis of established work on enabling formalisation, decision rights, proportional risk management, responsible innovation, high reliability and administrative burden. They are not presented as the first theory of enabling governance.

## Primary and authoritative evidence

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Playbook

NIST treats risk tolerance as an organisational decision connected to mission, strategy and context. The Playbook supports:

- determining the level of risk-management activity according to risk tolerance;
- allocating resources toward the most material risks;
- approval, conditional approval and disapproval;
- go/no-go decisions;
- oversight proportionate to the risk level;
- stakeholder feedback and independent course correction;
- risk responses including mitigation, transfer, avoidance and acceptance;
- superseding, disengaging or deactivating systems where performance or outcomes exceed tolerance or mitigation capacity.

**Evidence contribution:** governance need not apply one uniform process to every AI use. It can define differentiated pathways and legitimate operating space within established tolerances.

**Boundary:** the AI RMF is voluntary, context-sensitive and not a guarantee that a chosen risk tolerance is ethically, legally or strategically sound. NIST has indicated that AI RMF 1.0 is under revision, requiring a publication-stage currency check.

### Australian Government Guidance for AI Adoption

Current Australian guidance advises organisations to adapt practices to their size, use cases and risk profile; screen uses for unacceptable or heightened risk; apply controls proportionate to each use; and match human oversight to autonomy and stakes. It also supports clear intervention points, override, rollback and shutdown where appropriate.

**Evidence contribution:** proportionate governance can permit routine low-consequence activity while directing greater attention and review to more consequential or uncertain uses.

**Boundary:** proportionate does not mean minimal. Some uses may require prohibition, independent scrutiny or no-go decisions.

### OECD AI Principles and *Advancing Accountability in AI*

The OECD connects accountability to role, context and ability to act. Its guidance treats AI risk management as iterative and context-specific, with treatment commensurate to potential adverse impact. It also recognises that organisations may need to consider the risks of not using AI where it could provide important benefits or insights.

**Evidence contribution:** responsible governance must consider both action and non-action, and should adapt controls to context rather than treat avoidance of all risk as the objective.

**Boundary:** balancing the risks of use and non-use does not justify avoidable harm or override rights, legal obligations or ethical constraints.

### MIT CISR and IT-governance decision rights

MIT CISR and Weill and Ross define governance through decision rights and accountability aligned to organisational objectives. Their work presents governance as simultaneously empowering and controlling by clarifying who decides and who is answerable.

**Evidence contribution:** governance can enable action where decision rights are clear and connected to accountability.

**Boundary:** IT-governance theory predates contemporary AI and does not by itself address opacity, adaptive behaviour, human oversight or affected-party rights.

## Academic evidence

### Enabling and coercive formalisation

Adler and Borys distinguish two ways formalisation can be experienced and designed. Enabling formalisation supports people in dealing with contingencies and improving their work; coercive formalisation is oriented primarily toward compelling compliance and controlling effort.

Their framework identifies design features associated with enabling systems, including the capacity to repair a process, internal transparency about how it works, global transparency about how local work connects to the wider system and flexibility where conditions require adjustment.

**Evidence contribution:** the same degree of formalisation can have different consequences depending on how the system is designed and used.

**Boundary:** enabling and coercive are not simple labels attached permanently to a rule. Experience varies with hierarchy, incentives, safety, implementation and context.

### Controls that support flexibility and performance

Ahrens and Chapman show that management controls can support both efficiency and flexibility where they are used actively in operational work. Wouters and Wilderom demonstrate how participative development can make performance-measurement systems more enabling. Chapman and Kihn link integrated information systems with enabling control and performance.

**Evidence contribution:** control and flexibility are not inherent opposites. Formal systems can help people diagnose, coordinate and adjust work.

**Boundary:** field evidence from particular settings does not establish that any formal system will improve flexibility or performance.

### Hierarchy and the loss of enabling qualities

Cäker and Siverbo show that performance systems experienced as enabling at one level may become less enabling when used within hierarchical accountability. Research on budget control likewise indicates that managerial safety and context affect whether controls are experienced as useful or coercive.

**Evidence contribution:** governance cannot be classified as enabling from its written design alone. The way information is used by more powerful actors matters.

**Boundary:** hierarchy and accountability are not inherently coercive. They become problematic where they suppress repair, learning, challenge or legitimate discretion.

### Red tape, burden and workarounds

Van Loon, Leisink and Knies emphasise both compliance burden and whether a rule advances its functional objective. George, Pandey and Steijn's meta-analysis associates red tape with poorer organisational performance and employee outcomes. Bozeman, Youtie and Jung document how accumulations of small administrative tasks can drive workarounds and non-compliance.

**Evidence contribution:** governance burden must be judged not only by the number of requirements but by their usefulness, cumulative cost and relationship to the risk being controlled.

**Boundary:** burdensome does not mean unnecessary. Some high-consequence controls are legitimately demanding.

### High-reliability organising

High-reliability research emphasises preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience and deference to expertise. Empirical work also shows the importance of shared baselines and attention to weak signals.

**Evidence contribution:** reliable performance combines disciplined standards with adaptation, escalation and movement toward relevant expertise when conditions depart from the expected.

**Boundary:** high-reliability principles come largely from hazardous and specialised settings and should not be universalised mechanically across all AI uses.

### Responsible innovation

Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten organise responsible innovation around anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness. De Hoop, Pols and Romijn identify practical limits and blind spots, including unequal power, material constraints and situations where participation or reflection does not translate into action.

**Evidence contribution:** enabling governance needs mechanisms for learning, inclusion and change, not merely permission at the start of a project.

**Boundary:** responsible-innovation processes can become ceremonial and cannot resolve every value conflict or institutional constraint.

## Evidence by chapter domain

### 1. Enabling versus coercive governance

**Evidence establishes:**
- formalisation can support work or primarily compel compliance;
- enabling qualities include repair, transparency, system understanding and flexibility;
- experience depends on implementation, hierarchy and use of information.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
AI governance is more enabling where it helps people understand the boundary, solve problems, respond to exceptions and see how local decisions connect to organisational risk.

**Safe formulation:**
> The usefulness of governance depends not only on how much formal control exists, but on whether the control helps people perform, repair and adapt the work responsibly.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Formal governance is inherently bureaucratic or coercive.

### 2. Risk appetite and authorised boundaries

**Evidence establishes:**
- risk tolerance informs resource allocation, approval pathways and go/no-go decisions;
- roles and decision rights should be connected to accountability;
- risk levels and tolerances may change over the lifecycle.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
A usable risk appetite translates strategic intent into boundaries within which defined decisions can proceed without repeatedly returning to the highest authority.

**Safe formulation:**
> Risk appetite becomes useful when it is translated into decisions, thresholds, authority and escalation conditions that people can apply.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Risk appetite is permission to accept any risk that management prefers.

Legal duties, rights, safety and ethical limits may place risks outside legitimate appetite.

### 3. Proportionality

**Evidence establishes:**
- authoritative guidance recommends controls and oversight matched to risk, context, autonomy and stakes;
- scarce risk resources should be directed toward the most material issues;
- low-risk and high-risk uses need not follow identical pathways.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Proportionality is a design discipline: enough governance for the exposure, without treating every AI use as equally consequential.

**Safe formulation:**
> Proportionate governance varies the depth, independence, evidence and approval required according to the use and potential consequence.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Proportionality means reducing controls until a project can proceed.

### 4. Conditional approval and exceptions

**Evidence establishes:**
- governance frameworks recognise conditional approval, disapproval, escalation and course correction;
- enabling formalisation allows repair and adaptation within the system;
- responsible innovation requires responsiveness when assumptions change.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Governance can permit bounded progress while making conditions, evidence requirements, expiry, monitoring and triggers for renewed review explicit.

**Safe formulation:**
> Conditional approval can be a stronger control than indefinite delay or unqualified approval when its limits and review triggers are explicit.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Exceptions are harmless because they are documented.

An exception must have a reason, authority, scope, duration, evidence and review path.

### 5. Escalation and deference to expertise

**Evidence establishes:**
- high-reliability organising values sensitivity to operations and deference to relevant expertise;
- NIST supports empowered challenge and independent course correction;
- Chapter 7 evidence shows capable people need usable permission to act.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Escalation should move the decision toward the knowledge and authority needed for the actual condition rather than merely upward through hierarchy.

**Safe formulation:**
> Escalation is enabling when it connects uncertainty to relevant expertise and decision authority without making ordinary work impossible.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Every uncertainty should be escalated to a central committee.

### 6. Governance burden and workarounds

**Evidence establishes:**
- red tape combines burden with weak contribution to the rule's functional purpose;
- cumulative administrative tasks can encourage avoidance and workarounds;
- burdens can harm performance and employee outcomes.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Governance debt can accumulate when requirements remain after their purpose, risk or operating context changes, or when overlapping controls are added without rationalisation.

**Safe formulation:**
> Governance should be periodically tested for purpose, duplication, burden and whether it still changes the decision it was designed to govern.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Any control that slows work is unnecessary red tape.

### 7. Responsible experimentation

**Evidence establishes:**
- responsible innovation requires anticipation, reflection, inclusion and responsiveness;
- current AI guidance permits differentiated and conditional governance;
- risk management should consider risks and potential benefits in context.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
An organisation can create controlled spaces for experimentation where purpose, scope, data, affected parties, authority, evidence, stop conditions and learning are explicit.

**Safe formulation:**
> Governance can enable responsible experimentation by bounding exposure and making learning and stop conditions visible.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> A sandbox makes an experiment responsible.

### 8. Permission and institutional responsibility

**Evidence establishes:**
- governance allocates decision rights and accountability;
- risk frameworks retain responsible actors and lifecycle obligations;
- bounded authority remains subject to monitoring, challenge and withdrawal.

**Reasonable interpretation:**
Permission to proceed remains an institutional decision. It does not transfer responsibility to the user, team or AI system operating within the boundary.

**Safe formulation:**
> Permission defines legitimate operating room while preserving accountability for the boundary, its assumptions and its consequences.

**Unsafe formulation:**
> Once a use is approved within appetite, later outcomes are the responsibility of the operator or system.

## Challenge and contrary evidence

### Enabling governance can become coercive in use

A participative, transparent control may later be used for surveillance, blame or rigid performance comparison. Hierarchical accountability can change how a control is experienced.

**Implication:** assess governance in operation, not only by design intent.

### Clarity can harden the wrong boundary

A perfectly clear rule may encode a poor decision, exclude affected interests or become obsolete as conditions change.

**Implication:** clarity must be accompanied by review, challenge and responsiveness.

### Proportionality can be manipulated

Teams may understate consequence or uncertainty to qualify for a lighter pathway. Risk appetite may become a rhetorical device for approving commercially attractive activity.

**Implication:** material classifications need evidence, challenge and independent review where stakes warrant it.

### Exceptions can become the real system

Repeated exceptions may indicate that the rule is impractical, incentives are misaligned or the risk model is wrong. They can also normalise control erosion.

**Implication:** monitor the pattern and cumulative effect of exceptions, not only individual approvals.

### Too little governance can create hesitation rather than freedom

Where boundaries and authority are unclear, people may delay, seek unnecessary approval or act inconsistently because they do not know what is legitimate.

**Implication:** absence of process is not empowerment.

### Too much governance can create workarounds

Cumulative burdens and low-value approvals can push work into informal channels, weaken transparency and consume attention needed for material risks.

**Implication:** simplify by removing duplication and improving design, not by abandoning accountability.

### Responsible innovation has institutional limits

Anticipation and inclusion cannot resolve every conflict or overcome weak authority, resource constraints and commercial pressure.

**Implication:** retain decision rights, enforcement, no-go options and accountability.

### High-reliability principles are not universal recipes

Practices from aviation, healthcare or hazardous operations may be disproportionate for low-consequence uses.

**Implication:** borrow the logic of attention, expertise and resilience without copying every process.

## Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes

- formalisation can be enabling or coercive depending on design and use;
- well-designed controls can support flexibility, repair, transparency and performance;
- hierarchical use can weaken enabling qualities;
- red tape and cumulative burden can harm outcomes and encourage workarounds;
- risk management should be proportionate, contextual and connected to tolerance, approval and escalation;
- high-reliability organising combines disciplined baselines with attention, adaptation and expertise;
- responsible innovation requires responsiveness and has practical limits.

### Reasonable interpretation

- governance can create legitimate room to act where it clarifies boundaries and authority;
- risk appetite should become operational decision guidance rather than remain a statement;
- conditional approval and explicit exceptions can preserve both movement and control;
- governance burden should be evaluated against purpose and material risk;
- capable people need practical permission, not merely formal authority;
- permission must remain reviewable, revocable and institutionally accountable.

### Gardner synthesis

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act by clarifying what may proceed, within which boundaries, under whose authority, with what evidence and when escalation is required.**

> **The opposite of weak governance is not necessarily more governance. It is better-designed governance.**

> **Permission to act is not permission to ignore risk.**

## Proposed controlled definition

For human approval:

> **Governance as Permission is an organisational lens for designing governance so that people and systems can act within explicit, proportionate and reviewable boundaries, with defined authority, evidence requirements, exception paths, escalation and the ability to restrict or withdraw permission when conditions change.**

The formulation describes a book-level lens, not a validated instrument, legal doctrine, compliance status or universal governance model.

## Claims safe to use

- Formal governance can enable or constrain responsible work depending on design and use.
- Clear decision rights and accountability can create legitimate room for action.
- Risk tolerance and appetite should be translated into operational boundaries and escalation conditions.
- Governance should be proportionate to context, risk, autonomy and consequence.
- Conditional approval can be a legitimate governance outcome.
- Exception paths are part of control when they are authorised, bounded, evidenced and reviewed.
- Escalation should connect uncertainty to relevant expertise and authority.
- Red tape is not synonymous with all formal control; burden and functional purpose both matter.
- Cumulative governance burden can create delay, workarounds and weaker transparency.
- High-reliability organising combines standards with attention to anomalies and deference to expertise.
- Responsible experimentation requires boundaries, evidence, feedback and stop conditions.
- Permission remains reviewable, revocable and institutionally accountable.

## Claims requiring qualification

- Do not claim governance automatically increases speed, innovation, trust, ROI or adoption.
- Do not claim less governance is inherently more enabling.
- Do not equate employee discretion with absence of control.
- Do not treat risk appetite as authority to ignore law, rights, safety or ethical limits.
- Do not assume that a sandbox or pilot is responsible merely because exposure is limited.
- Do not treat every exception as a healthy sign of flexibility.
- Do not universalise high-reliability practices from hazardous sectors.
- Do not present enabling governance as a stable property that can be inferred from policy wording alone.
- Do not imply that approval transfers responsibility away from the institution.

## Narrative opportunity

### Opening tension

Use a composite organisation with a capable cross-functional team proposing a low-consequence AI use within a well-understood customer-service process.

The organisation has built substantial governance after an earlier incident. Every change now enters the same approval route regardless of consequence. The proposed use has bounded data, no autonomous external action, clear human ownership and an easy reversal path. Nevertheless, it requires sequential review by privacy, security, legal, risk, architecture, procurement and an executive committee.

After several weeks, the approval remains pending. The business team begins using a less visible manual workaround supported by an unapproved general-purpose tool because the formal pathway cannot distinguish this modest use from a materially consequential system.

Nothing is wrong with having controls. The problem is that the governance system cannot say what is allowed.

Opening question:

> **What is governance for if it can identify risk but cannot create a legitimate path to act?**

### Reader discovery

The reader should discover that governance does not enable action by becoming weaker. It enables action by making clear:

- what can proceed;
- which boundary applies;
- who may decide;
- what evidence is required;
- what exceptions are legitimate;
- when escalation is necessary;
- when permission expires or must be withdrawn.

### Dominant proposition

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act.**

This must be immediately bounded by the fuller formulation and the explicit statement that permission to act is not permission to ignore risk.

### Ending movement

End after the organisation has a defined, proportionate pathway and can act with greater legitimacy — but cannot yet prove that the pathway operated as intended.

The final movement is:

> **Permission without evidence becomes optimism.**

That leads directly to Chapter 9 — *Evidence Before Assurance*.
