**Date:** 2026-11-18  
**Status reviewed:** Developmental Review

## Overall judgement

The v0.2 rewrite resolves the structural and book-prose failures identified in v0.1.

The chapter now advances the whole-book argument from Chapter 3's individual decision tension to an organisational assurance question: what does a board or governing body actually know when it is told that consequential AI decisions receive human review?

The five-condition diagnostic is now earned through sustained narrative and evidence. The chapter does not need another developmental rewrite.

Three limited citation-visibility repairs are required before the evidence gate can receive an unqualified PASS:

1. the context-dependent automation-effects paragraph needs visible human-factors citation;
2. the accountability-alignment paragraph needs visible Siebert/OECD citation;
3. the close-prior-art acknowledgement needs visible citations to the related frameworks it describes.

These gaps do not alter the argument or framework. They require a targeted v0.3 Author Revision.

## Gate 1 — Proposition

**Outcome: PASS**

The governing proposition is clear, useful and sustained:

> **Accountability should not outrun capability, visibility, authority or intervention capacity.**

The chapter explains why formal role assignment, training and override rights are necessary but insufficient. It preserves wider executive and institutional responsibility rather than reducing accountability to direct operational control.

## Gate 2 — Architecture

**Outcome: PASS**

The chapter progresses coherently:

1. board-level assurance that human review exists;
2. inability to demonstrate whether human judgement is usable;
3. established relationship among ability, authority, responsibility and control;
4. capability;
5. visibility;
6. authority;
7. intervention capacity;
8. accountability alignment;
9. formal Meaningful Accountability Test;
10. diagnostic-not-certificate boundary;
11. transition to delegated AI authority.

The framework does not appear before the reader has encountered the organisational problem.

## Chapter-to-Chapter Novelty

**Outcome: PASS**

The new opening no longer repeats Chapter 3's manager-at-the-decision-screen scene.

- Chapter 3 asks whether the named human still has meaningful practical control.
- Chapter 4 asks whether the organisation can substantiate its assurance that human review preserves accountability.

The return to human review changes scale and meaning. It advances from an individual decision problem to an organisational governance diagnostic.

## Gate 3 — Evidence

**Outcome: CONDITIONAL PASS**

The evidence architecture is strong and materially survives in the manuscript.

### Evidence visible and correctly bounded

- Flemisch and Cavalcante Siebert support the relationship among ability, authority, responsibility and control.
- EU and Australian guidance support competence, training, authority and support.
- Sterz supports epistemic access and causal power.
- Lee, See and Zhang support the limits of disclosure, explanation and trust calibration.
- NIST and OECD support delegated authority, empowerment and role/context-based accountability.
- Nitchals and van de Sande support temporal capacity, intervention power and intervention effectiveness.
- Green and Laux provide the institutional red-team challenge.

### Required citation-visibility repairs

#### E4-01 — Context-dependent automation effects

The manuscript states:

> “Human-factors research shows, however, that the outcome depends on task design, system reliability, workload, engagement and the conditions under which intervention is required.”

This is supported in the evidence register but lacks a visible manuscript citation.

**Required repair:** cite Parasuraman & Riley and/or Onnasch et al. at the end of the paragraph.

#### E4-02 — Accountability alignment

The manuscript states:

> “Meaningful-human-control scholarship argues that responsibility attributed to a person should be commensurate with their ability and authority. The OECD's accountability principle likewise looks to role, context and ability to act.”

The claim is well supported but the paragraph lacks visible citation.

**Required repair:** cite Cavalcante Siebert et al. and OECD.

#### E4-03 — Close-prior-art acknowledgement

The manuscript states:

> “Closely related contemporary models set out conditions for effective oversight and meaningful human authority.”

This is an important provenance statement, but the related frameworks are not cited at that point.

**Required repair:** cite Sterz et al., Nitchals and van de Sande et al.

## Gate 4 — Narrative

**Outcome: PASS**

The board paper creates a recognisable organisational tension without relying on crisis or futurism. Curiosity deepens as the assurance claim is tested against what reviewers can actually know, decide and change.

The board thread returns at the visibility section and after the formal test, giving the chapter continuity without forcing the example into every paragraph.

The chapter does not explain the framework before the reader has experienced the problem.

## Gate 5 — Book-Prose Continuity

**Outcome: PASS**

The v0.1 framework/article cadence has been materially repaired.

Most paragraphs now develop complete movements. The short line:

> **Capability does not confer authority.**

is justified as a scarce and earned proposition.

The five diagnostic questions are presented as one deliberate numbered list after the framework has been developed. In this context the list improves comprehension and does not control the chapter's overall page shape.

The chapter reads as book prose rather than an extended article, presentation or consulting handout.

## Gate 6 — Theory, Authenticity and Provenance

**Outcome: PASS**

The manuscript clearly states that the component ideas are established rather than newly discovered.

The contribution is accurately limited to:

- concise organisational synthesis;
- accountability as the organising question;
- linkage to the Accountability–Control Gap;
- application to a person, role, team or organisation;
- integration into the governance operating system.

The test is not presented as a validated instrument, universal legal standard, compliance certificate or first-of-kind framework.

The distinction among the five conditions is coherent:

- capability concerns evaluative competence;
- visibility concerns decision-relevant access;
- authority concerns legitimate and protected standing;
- intervention capacity concerns practical, timely causal power;
- accountability alignment concerns proportionality and legibility of responsibility.

## Red-Team Intellectual Challenge

**Outcome: PASS**

The chapter retains the strongest challenges:

- human involvement is not automatically beneficial;
- automation effects vary by task and context;
- transparency does not guarantee understanding;
- a system can preserve meaningful accountability and remain unsuitable;
- individual oversight must be supported by institutional design;
- distributed accountability does not remove executive responsibility for delegation and governance design.

The chapter does not use the test to guarantee safety or justify deployment.

## Gate 7 — Reader Engrossment

**Outcome: PASS**

The reader is invited to doubt a familiar green assurance statement and gradually discovers why it is incomplete. The chapter offers enough intellectual delay before naming the test.

The ending creates forward pressure without summarising the lesson:

> **What authority has the organisation actually delegated to the AI?**

## Practical value

**Outcome: PASS**

The chapter changes the leadership question from:

> Who is accountable?

To:

> Has the organisation made that accountability exercisable in practice?

It improves judgement without becoming a mechanical assessment manual.

## Voice

**Outcome: PASS**

The prose is practical, evidence-aware, commercially grounded and quietly sceptical of ceremonial governance. It retains an engineering concern for how formal arrangements behave under real operating conditions.
