# Chapter 1 — The AI You Are Already Governing

## Research question
How widely and invisibly is AI already embedded in organisational work, and what does an organisation need to see before it can govern AI meaningfully?

## Core claims to verify
- AI is often embedded in products, workflows and vendor systems before organisations create formal governance.
- A simple tool inventory is insufficient because governance depends on understanding the **work, decision or action** that AI influences.
- Risk depends on context of use, consequence and decision participation, not merely on the presence of an AI feature.
- Visibility is a prerequisite for meaningful governance.

## Primary / authoritative evidence
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — organisational AI management system and identifying AI use.
- NIST AI RMF / Playbook — context mapping, intended purpose, actors and impacts.
- OECD AI Principles — lifecycle responsibility and traceability.
- Australian Guidance for AI Adoption — identifying AI use and maintaining accountability.
- Relevant government or regulator guidance on AI inventories/use-case registers.

## Academic evidence
- socio-technical systems literature;
- technology-in-practice / work-system literature;
- shadow IT and shadow AI research;
- embedded algorithmic decision support;
- organisational AI adoption studies.

## Contrary / boundary test
- Not every embedded AI feature creates material governance risk.
- Excessive inventory requirements can generate bureaucracy without improving control.
- Some low-risk AI use may be safely governed through general controls rather than bespoke AI governance.

## Case/example needs
- AI embedded in a familiar enterprise platform without a separate “AI project”.
- Employee use of public generative AI outside formal procurement.
- A low-risk use and a consequential use of the same underlying AI capability.

## Gardner synthesis boundary
Original contribution should be the move from **AI inventory** to **decision/use visibility**:
> Govern the work AI participates in, not merely the tools labelled AI.

Do not claim that use-case inventories or shadow AI are new ideas.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Chapter 1 can be drafted when:
- at least 2 primary governance sources establish the need for use/context visibility;
- at least 2 credible organisational/adoption sources show embedded or informal AI use;
- one contrary source or implementation critique tests over-inventory;
- 2–3 verified cases are available;
- key terms are fixed: AI use, embedded AI, shadow AI, consequential use, decision participation.

# Chapter 2 — When Policy Exists but Control Does Not

## Research question
What distinguishes declared AI governance from governance that changes organisational behaviour and control?

## Core claims to verify
- Policies and principles are necessary but insufficient for operational governance.
- Governance becomes real through roles, decision rights, controls, evidence, escalation and continual improvement.
- Organisations can formalise responsible-AI structures while still experiencing implementation gaps.

## Primary / authoritative evidence
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023;
- NIST AI RMF Govern function;
- OECD accountability/traceability;
- authoritative internal-control frameworks where relevant.

## Academic / institutional evidence
- policy-practice gap;
- decoupling and symbolic compliance;
- organisational implementation research;
- responsible-AI maturity research.

## Contrary / boundary test
- Formal policies can materially change behaviour when backed by leadership, incentives and controls.
- Avoid implying that all governance structures are theatre.

## Case/example needs
- organisation with strong principles but weak implementation;
- organisation where a policy triggered operational controls or changed practice.

## Gardner synthesis boundary
> Governance exists where principles change decisions, permissions, controls, evidence and behaviour.

Treat this as synthesis, not empirical law.

## Safe-to-draft gate
At least 2 primary frameworks + 2 implementation studies + one policy-success counterexample.

# Chapter 3 — The Human Who Still Owns the Decision

## Research question
What happens when responsibility remains human but practical control over the decision has migrated toward automation?

## Core claims to verify
- Humans can be assigned responsibility despite limited control.
- Automation can reduce situation awareness and intervention effectiveness.
- Accountability must be examined across distributed socio-technical systems.

## Primary / authoritative evidence
- current human-control guidance from Australian Government and other authoritative frameworks;
- OECD accountability principles.

## Academic anchors
- Elish — moral crumple zones;
- Endsley & Kiris — out-of-the-loop performance;
- meaningful human control;
- responsibility-gap literature;
- distributed agency/accountability.

## Contrary / boundary test
- responsibility can legitimately be distributed rather than concentrated;
- humans may retain meaningful accountability despite automation if controls are well designed;
- not every automation creates a responsibility gap.

## Case/example needs
- one historical automation case;
- one current AI-assisted professional decision;
- one case showing responsibility properly distributed.

## Gardner synthesis
**Accountability–Control Gap**

Definition to test:
> The mismatch between accountability assigned to a person or role and the practical control that person or role can realistically exercise.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Original literature verified; at least one current AI application case; construct provenance completed.

# Chapter 4 — The Conditions for Meaningful Accountability

## Research question
What practical conditions must be present for human accountability over AI-enabled work to be meaningful?

## Core claims to verify
- final authority without capability is insufficient;
- meaningful oversight requires information, competence and intervention capacity;
- human presence does not guarantee human agency;
- the required level of oversight is context-dependent.

## Primary / authoritative evidence
- Australian Guidance for AI Adoption — meaningful human control and override;
- Australian AI Reference Architecture — human agency, oversight and control;
- OECD accountability;
- relevant NIST governance/control guidance.

## Academic anchors
- meaningful human control: ability, authority, responsibility;
- human factors and situation awareness;
- automation bias;
- trust calibration;
- expertise and skill maintenance.

## Contrary / boundary test
- per-action human approval can create bottlenecks or poorer outcomes;
- some control can be designed ex ante rather than exercised synchronously;
- humans can introduce error as well as correct AI error.

## Gardner synthesis
**Meaningful Accountability Test**
1. Capability
2. Visibility
3. Authority
4. Intervention capacity
5. Accountability alignment

## Safe-to-draft gate
Each of the five dimensions must have independent evidentiary support and at least one boundary condition.

# Chapter 5 — What Have We Actually Allowed the AI to Do?

## Research question
How should organisations allocate decision and action authority between humans and AI?

## Core claims to verify
- not all decisions should have the same human/AI allocation;
- ambiguity, consequence, reversibility and risk matter;
- decision-making includes framing, acting and learning;
- AI authority should be explicit rather than accidental.

## Primary / strong evidence
- MIT CISR 2026 Designing Decision Rights for AI;
- OECD/NIST governance sources;
- sector guidance on autonomous systems where useful.

## Academic evidence
- decision-rights theory;
- delegation;
- autonomy levels;
- organisational design;
- decision support versus automation.

## Contrary / boundary test
- simplistic autonomy ladders can hide task/context differences;
- humans can be slower or less reliable for routine low-risk decisions;
- different stages of one decision may justify different AI authority.

## Case/example needs
- One NZ or another verified enterprise case;
- high-risk/low-ambiguity decision;
- low-risk/high-ambiguity decision.

## Gardner synthesis
Develop **Delegated Authority Map** only after source mapping. Likely axes:
- consequence;
- ambiguity;
- reversibility;
- scope of action;
- required human judgement.

Do not duplicate MIT's matrix.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Distinctiveness review completed against MIT and neighbouring frameworks.

# Chapter 6 — Where Governance Actually Lives

## Research question
Which operational and technical mechanisms make governance real inside organisational systems?

## Core claims to verify
- permissions, data, identity, logging, workflow design and configuration operationalise governance;
- vendor and third-party controls are part of organisational accountability;
- technical controls can encode authorised boundaries.

## Primary sources
- NIST AI RMF;
- ISO/IEC 42001;
- Australian cyber guidance;
- privacy/security guidance;
- current agentic AI harness guidance.

## Evidence domains
- data governance and provenance;
- identity/access management;
- least privilege;
- vendor risk;
- model/system monitoring;
- logging and auditability.

## Contrary / boundary test
- technical controls cannot substitute for organisational judgement;
- too much centralisation can make governance brittle;
- controls can produce false assurance if poorly monitored.

## Case/example needs
- permission boundary preventing unauthorised action;
- vendor dependency creating governance blind spot;
- logging enabling reconstruction of an incident.

## Gardner synthesis boundary
Governance is partly **encoded in infrastructure**, but is not reducible to technical architecture.

## Safe-to-draft gate
At least one authoritative source for each major control family; no unsupported technical claims.

# Chapter 7 — Capability Before Compliance

## Research question
When does human capability become a governance control rather than simply a training concern?

## Core claims to verify
- people need sufficient competence to interpret limitations and know when to intervene;
- automation can alter skill and situation awareness;
- trust must be calibrated rather than maximised;
- professional judgement can weaken through over-reliance.

## Primary / authoritative sources
- Australian human-control guidance;
- relevant regulatory literacy/competence requirements;
- NIST governance/training recommendations.

## Academic anchors
- Endsley & Kiris;
- automation bias;
- algorithm aversion/appreciation;
- trust calibration;
- expertise;
- skill atrophy/cognitive offloading;
- human-AI team performance.

## Contrary / boundary test
- AI can improve novice performance in some tasks;
- retained manual skill is not equally important in every context;
- excessive scepticism can reduce value just as over-trust can.

## Case/example needs
- professional judgement preserved by AI;
- skill erosion or over-reliance;
- effective trust-calibration practice.

## Gardner synthesis
> AI literacy becomes governance capability when it determines whether a person can recognise, question and intervene in consequential AI-supported work.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Use systematic reviews/meta-analyses where available; avoid anecdotal deskilling claims.

# Chapter 8 — Governance as Permission

## Research question
Under what conditions can governance enable action rather than merely constrain it?

## Core claims to verify
- formalisation can be enabling or coercive depending on design;
- clarity of boundaries and escalation can support action;
- proportional controls may reduce uncertainty;
- more governance is not necessarily better governance.

## Academic anchors
- Adler & Borys — enabling versus coercive bureaucracy;
- organisational control theory;
- high-reliability organisations;
- risk appetite;
- psychological safety where relevant;
- enabling controls.

## Practitioner / institutional evidence
- ISO/IEC 42001 on managing risks and opportunities;
- responsible innovation frameworks;
- case evidence linking clear governance to adoption confidence.

## Contrary / boundary test
- governance can create delay, bureaucracy and false confidence;
- causal claims about speed, innovation or ROI are not established;
- enabling effects depend on design and context.

## Case/example needs
- governance enabling a team to proceed safely;
- governance process creating avoidable friction;
- proportionate control redesign.

## Gardner synthesis
**Governance as Permission**

Safe claim:
> Well-designed governance can create conditions for confident action by clarifying boundaries, authority and escalation.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Strong enabling-control evidence + at least one credible failure/friction case. No causal performance claim without stronger evidence.

# Chapter 9 — Evidence Before Assurance

## Research question
What evidence must an organisation retain to demonstrate that AI governance actually operated as intended?

## Core claims to verify
- traceability is central to accountability;
- assurance requires reconstructable evidence;
- monitoring and exceptions are part of governance;
- documented process alone does not prove control effectiveness.

## Primary / authoritative sources
- OECD traceability;
- NIST documentation/monitoring;
- ISO/IEC 42001 continual improvement;
- audit/internal-control standards;
- AI assurance guidance.

## Academic / professional evidence
- assurance;
- auditability;
- model risk management;
- continuous monitoring;
- incident learning;
- internal control effectiveness.

## Contrary / boundary test
- more evidence can create documentation burden without better assurance;
- not all AI uses require the same evidence depth;
- transparency and auditability can conflict with privacy/security.

## Gardner synthesis
Evolved Governance Chain:
**Purpose/use → delegated authority → risk/exposure → required human judgement → controls → accountable owner → evidence → monitoring → intervention/learning**

Treat as diagnostic traceability model, not universal lifecycle sequence.

## Safe-to-draft gate
Each link in chain mapped to established evidence/control concepts; non-linearity caveat retained.

# Chapter 10 — When the System Begins to Act

## Research question
How does governance change when AI systems can plan, use tools and initiate actions with limited human involvement?

## Core claims to verify
- agentic AI introduces additional governance risks through autonomy, tool use and system access;
- identity, privilege, monitoring, reversibility and human control become central;
- governance must address delegated action authority rather than only output quality;
- “agentic” is multidimensional, not binary.

## Primary / current sources
- NIST Agent Standards Initiative/current NIST agent work;
- ASD/Cyber Security guidance on agentic AI and harnesses;
- Australian Government agentic AI standard/addendum;
- current relevant international guidance.

## Academic anchors
- Nature 2026 agentic profiles;
- autonomy and multi-agent systems;
- delegation and accountability;
- human supervisory control.

## Contrary / boundary test
- not all agents are highly autonomous;
- some risks are conventional IAM/cybersecurity problems rather than new AI problems;
- greater autonomy can be appropriate for low-risk, reversible tasks.

## Case/example needs
- agent with bounded privileges;
- action chain that crosses systems;
- incident or near-miss involving excessive permissions;
- well-designed agentic deployment.

## Gardner synthesis
The governance question shifts from:
> Who approved this output?

to:
> What organisational authority did we delegate to this system, and under what conditions can that authority be constrained, revoked or overridden?

## Safe-to-draft gate
Current sources rechecked immediately before drafting and again before publication because this chapter will age fastest.

# Case-study control

Every named case must record:
- organisation;
- date and jurisdiction;
- source;
- what actually happened;
- precise proposition the case supports;
- limitations;
- whether the organisation approved or published the account;
- whether the case is used as evidence or illustration.

Do not use a consulting anecdote as if it proves a general proposition.

# Chapter drafting release rule

A chapter moves from **Research** to **Safe to Draft** only when:

1. material claims have stable IDs;
2. primary/authoritative sources have been opened;
3. academic support is sufficient for empirical/theoretical claims;
4. at least one meaningful contrary or boundary source has been tested;
5. cases are verified;
6. Gardner synthesis is explicitly separated from sourced evidence;
7. unresolved gaps are recorded;
8. fast-moving sources are current enough for drafting;
9. no provenance conflict remains that could change the chapter's central argument.
