**Date:** 2026-11-19

## Chapter role in the book

Chapter 7 established that capability is part of the governance system. People cannot exercise meaningful accountability merely because they have completed training, been assigned responsibility or been given a formal escalation route. They need the practical ability and organisational permission to recognise, judge, challenge, escalate and intervene.

Chapter 8 now asks whether the governance system itself creates legitimate room for that capability to be used.

The chapter must move the book from:

> capable people operating inside approved controls

To:

> **an organisation that can say what may proceed, within which boundary, under whose authority, with what evidence and when the decision must be escalated, restricted or reversed.**

This chapter is not an argument for less governance. It is an argument against governance that can only delay, prohibit or demand more information without defining a legitimate path to action.

The central intellectual movement is:

> **Control becomes enabling when it converts organisational intent and risk appetite into usable permission.**

The chapter must make clear that permission remains bounded, monitored, reviewable, revocable and institutionally accountable.

## Approved construct and provenance

### Name

**Governance as Permission**

### Controlled definition

> **Governance as Permission is an organisational lens for designing governance so that people and systems can act within explicit, proportionate and reviewable boundaries, with defined authority, evidence requirements, exception paths, escalation and the ability to restrict or withdraw permission when conditions change.**

### Governing propositions

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act by clarifying what may proceed, within which boundaries, under whose authority, with what evidence and when escalation is required.**

> **The opposite of weak governance is not necessarily more governance. It is better-designed governance.**

> **Permission to act is not permission to ignore risk.**

These are Gardner book-level synthesis informed by established prior work. They must not be presented as a newly discovered universal theory, a validated instrument, a legal doctrine, a maturity model or a compliance status.

## Reader entry belief

The reader may enter the chapter believing that serious governance is naturally cautious and that additional approval is usually safer than allowing local discretion.

A related belief is that governance becomes stronger as organisations add:

- more sign-offs;
- more committees;
- more policies;
- more templates;
- more evidence requests;
- more central review;
- fewer exceptions.

The reader may also assume that the alternative to heavy governance is weak governance: enthusiastic experimentation, informal workarounds and uncontrolled tool use.

The chapter must unsettle this binary.

Governance can fail through insufficient control. It can also fail through indiscriminate control that makes legitimate action difficult, consumes scarce scrutiny on low-consequence uses, drives activity into informal channels and leaves nobody certain about what the organisation actually permits.

## Opening tension

Open inside a composite organisation that strengthened its AI governance after an earlier problem. Do not describe the earlier event dramatically or in enough detail to create a second case study. It is sufficient that leaders responded by creating one central route for every AI proposal.

A cross-functional operations team now proposes an internal AI-supported knowledge assistant.

The proposed use is deliberately modest:

- it searches an approved internal repository of current project procedures and completed lessons-learned reports;
- it produces a first-pass summary with links back to the source material;
- it has no access to customer records, financial systems or employee performance data;
- it cannot send messages, approve work, alter records or act outside the repository;
- a named manager remains responsible for how the summary is used;
- the use can be disabled without interrupting the underlying process;
- the initial pilot is limited to one team and a defined period.

Yet the proposal enters the same sequential approval route used for materially consequential AI systems. It moves through privacy, cybersecurity, legal, enterprise risk, architecture, procurement, records management and an executive committee. Each function asks a reasonable question. None has authority to approve the bounded use. Several request evidence that can only be produced after a pilot. The executive committee meets monthly.

Weeks pass.

The team still needs to prepare operational summaries. Some staff begin copying selected material into an unapproved general-purpose chatbot. Others continue manually, producing inconsistent results. The organisation has not consciously chosen either approach. Its formal system has produced a vacuum in which the visible proposal remains blocked while less visible activity continues.

The opening question is:

> **What is governance for if it can identify risk but cannot create a legitimate path to act?**

The case must remain ordinary. Nobody should be portrayed as reckless, obstructive or foolish. Every function is trying to protect the organisation. The failure emerges from the combined design.

## Dominant proposition

> **Well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act.**

This short formulation may appear only after the reader has experienced the approval vacuum. It must then be immediately bounded by the fuller proposition and by:

> **Permission to act is not permission to ignore risk.**

The chapter must show that permission is produced through governance, not offered instead of governance.

## Primary reader discovery

The reader should discover that a governance system is incomplete if it can describe risks but cannot convert them into usable organisational decisions.

By the end of the chapter, the reader should understand that enabling governance must be able to distinguish among:

- permitted routine use;
- permitted use subject to defined controls;
- conditional or time-limited approval;
- activity requiring escalation or independent review;
- prohibited use;
- permission that must be narrowed, paused or withdrawn.

The reader should stop asking only:

> What controls do we have?

and begin asking:

> **What legitimate action do these controls enable, and what decision do they prevent?**

## Terminology and conceptual boundaries

### Permission

Permission means bounded organisational authority to proceed under defined conditions. It is not personal discretion detached from policy or control.

Permission must be connected to:

- purpose;
- scope;
- decision owner;
- authority;
- permitted data and actions;
- controls;
- evidence;
- monitoring;
- escalation;
- expiry, review or revocation.

### Proportionality

Proportionality means matching the depth, independence, evidence and approval required to the use, uncertainty, autonomy and possible consequence.

Do not use proportionality as shorthand for lighter governance or convenience. A proportionate response to some uses is prohibition or independent review.

### Risk appetite and risk tolerance

Use the terms carefully. Risk appetite is the broad amount and type of risk an organisation is prepared to pursue or retain in seeking its objectives. Risk tolerance translates this into more specific acceptable variation, exposure or limits.

The chapter need not teach enterprise-risk terminology. Its purpose is to show that appetite and tolerance become useful only when translated into decisions people can apply.

### Conditional approval

Conditional approval means permission to proceed within named conditions, limits, evidence requirements and review triggers. It is not diluted approval or a polite way to avoid deciding.

### Exception

An exception is a formally authorised departure from a defined rule or normal pathway. It requires a reason, decision owner, scope, duration, evidence and review. Repeated exceptions may reveal a defective rule or control erosion.

### Red tape and governance burden

Do not label every slow or demanding control as red tape. The relevant distinction combines burden with weak contribution to the control's legitimate purpose.

Use **governance burden** as ordinary language for the time, effort and coordination required by governance. Use **governance debt** cautiously as Gardner descriptive language for accumulated, overlapping or obsolete requirements that remain after their purpose or context has changed. Do not introduce it as a new named framework.

## Narrative movement

### Movement 1 — A proposal with no legitimate route

Develop the opening case slowly enough for the reader to feel why the process appears reasonable.

Each review function should ask a defensible question:

- Privacy asks whether personal information is present.
- Cybersecurity asks how access and credentials are controlled.
- Legal asks about confidentiality and intellectual property.
- Architecture asks how the tool integrates with approved systems.
- Procurement asks about vendor terms and service continuity.
- Records management asks what is retained.
- Risk asks how the use has been classified.

The problem is not the questions. It is that the organisation has no differentiated decision route and no point at which the answers become sufficient.

Let the workaround emerge as a consequence of unresolved work, not as a moral failure. The visible process and the actual process begin to separate.

### Movement 2 — Governance that can only say no

Move from the case to the organisational paradox.

A governance system may contain many controls and still fail to govern because it cannot produce a timely decision. Delay itself may become an unrecorded decision: the proposal does not proceed formally, but the underlying need remains and activity shifts elsewhere.

Connect lightly to Chapter 2's distinction between policy and control without repeating it. The new contribution is that even enforceable control is incomplete where it cannot establish a legitimate operating boundary.

The chapter should state that absence of permission does not necessarily produce absence of action. It may produce:

- hesitation;
- repeated referral;
- inconsistent local decisions;
- shadow use;
- workarounds;
- reduced visibility;
- concentration of approval at levels unable to assess routine work.

Do not claim that delay always causes shadow AI. Present it as a foreseeable organisational response where legitimate work remains unresolved.

### Movement 3 — Formalisation can enable or coerce

Introduce Adler and Borys after the reader has seen that formal control is not the enemy.

Explain that formalisation may be designed and experienced in different ways. Enabling systems help people:

- understand how the process works;
- see how local work connects to wider organisational purposes;
- diagnose and repair problems;
- respond to contingencies without abandoning the control objective.

Coercive systems primarily compel adherence, concentrate knowledge and make local deviation evidence of failure.

Use Ahrens and Chapman, Wouters and Wilderom, and Chapman and Kihn to show that formal control can support flexibility and coordination. Use Cäker and Siverbo and the managerial-safety evidence to preserve the challenge: a system designed as enabling can become coercive through hierarchical use, surveillance or blame.

Do not create a binary scorecard or imply every control is purely one type.

The reader should reach the conclusion:

> The question is not whether governance is formal. The question is what the form enables people to understand and do.

### Movement 4 — Risk appetite must become permission

Return to the composite organisation's approved risk language. The board may have declared a moderate appetite for controlled experimentation and a low appetite for privacy, safety or legal harm. Yet no operational pathway converts those statements into a decision about this modest proposal.

Use NIST to explain that risk tolerance should influence:

- the level of risk-management activity;
- allocation of oversight resources;
- approval, conditional approval and disapproval;
- go/no-go decisions;
- monitoring and decommissioning.

Use OECD and Australian guidance to establish context-specific and proportionate risk management.

The Gardner interpretation is:

> A risk appetite that cannot be translated into decision rights, thresholds, evidence and escalation is a statement of preference, not yet a permission system.

Do not describe risk appetite as authority to trade away legal duties, rights, safety or legitimacy.

### Movement 5 — Proportionality is a governance discipline

Show why one approval route for every AI use appears fair and consistent but may produce poor control.

Uniform process can conceal material differences among uses. The internal knowledge assistant differs from a system that:

- ranks job applicants;
- makes eligibility recommendations;
- sends external communications;
- alters customer records;
- initiates financial transactions;
- affects physical safety.

Do not turn this into a list of risk tiers. The narrative point is that the organisation must examine the actual work, authority and consequence rather than govern the word *AI* as one undifferentiated category.

Explain that proportionate governance can vary:

- who may approve;
- which functions must review;
- how independent the review must be;
- what evidence is required before and after approval;
- whether a pilot is permitted;
- the monitoring cadence;
- the expiry or reassessment trigger.

Preserve the hostile case: teams may understate risk to obtain a lighter path. Material classifications therefore require evidence and challenge, especially where interests are strong.

### Movement 6 — Conditional approval is a decision

Develop conditional approval as the first practical expression of permission.

The team may be permitted to run a sixty-day internal pilot subject to conditions such as:

- use of the approved repository only;
- no personal or customer information;
- source links retained in every summary;
- no autonomous external action;
- named manager ownership;
- defined user group;
- logging of queries and material corrections;
- review after a stated volume of use or at the end of the period;
- automatic suspension if access, data scope or functionality changes.

The conditions must serve the actual risk. Do not create a generic control checklist.

Explain why conditional approval may be stronger than either open-ended approval or indefinite delay. It converts uncertainty into a bounded learning arrangement while preserving the right to stop.

The chapter must also say plainly that some proposals should not receive conditional approval. No-go remains a legitimate governance outcome.

### Movement 7 — Exceptions belong inside governance

Use an operational moment from the pilot. The knowledge assistant encounters a document type outside its approved source set or produces a summary where the source material conflicts. The ordinary rule is insufficient.

An enabling governance system does not force the user either to proceed blindly or abandon the task. It provides an exception path:

- pause the output;
- identify the condition;
- refer it to the relevant owner or expert;
- record the decision;
- decide whether the exception remains singular or requires a change to the rule.

Show that an exception is not an escape hatch from governance. It is a governed response to a condition that the normal rule did not resolve.

Preserve the challenge that repeated exceptions can become the real system. The organisation should examine patterns, cumulative exposure and whether the original boundary remains workable.

### Movement 8 — Escalation should move towards expertise

Use high-reliability organising to explain why escalation should not simply move upward through hierarchy.

The useful question is:

> Who has the knowledge and authority required by this condition?

For some issues, that may be a privacy specialist, records manager, engineer, domain expert or executive decision owner. Seniority alone is not expertise.

Use sensitivity to operations, reluctance to simplify and deference to expertise as supporting concepts. Do not import the full high-reliability model or claim that ordinary office AI uses require aviation-style controls.

The organisational lesson is that ordinary work should remain ordinary. Escalation should be reserved for conditions that exceed the permitted boundary, and it should connect the issue to someone able to decide.

### Movement 9 — The burden test

Return to the original approval route and examine its cumulative cost.

Each review step may be defensible in isolation. Together they may duplicate questions, require repeated explanation and leave no accountable integrator. This is where governance burden becomes material.

Use van Loon and colleagues to distinguish compliance burden from rule functionality. Use George, Pandey and Steijn for evidence on employee and organisational outcomes. Use Bozeman, Youtie and Jung to show how accumulations of small administrative tasks can produce workarounds and non-compliance.

The chapter should not argue for measuring governance only by speed. A difficult process may be justified by a serious risk. Instead, ask:

- Does this requirement address a material risk?
- Does it change the decision?
- Is the same issue reviewed elsewhere?
- Is the evidence available at this stage?
- Does the person requesting it have decision authority?
- What happens when the requirement is satisfied?
- Does the control remain necessary as the use changes?

Use these as prose questions, not as a visible audit checklist.

Introduce the descriptive idea of governance debt only if it improves the paragraph: requirements can accumulate as reactions to earlier events and remain after their context changes. Do not capitalise or brand the term.

### Movement 10 — Responsible experimentation

Show the pilot as a form of governed learning rather than a sandbox theatre exercise.

Use responsible-innovation scholarship to bring in:

- anticipation of plausible effects;
- reflection on assumptions and values;
- inclusion of people affected by the work;
- responsiveness when evidence changes.

Preserve the critique from de Hoop, Pols and Romijn. Participation and reflection do not guarantee responsible outcomes. Power, resources, commercial pressure and weak authority may prevent adaptation.

The pilot is responsible only if it has:

- a legitimate purpose;
- bounded exposure;
- named decision ownership;
- appropriate participants;
- evidence collection;
- usable feedback;
- stop and change conditions;
- an actual decision at the end.

Do not imply that use of the word *pilot* or *sandbox* makes activity safe or legitimate.

### Movement 11 — Permission remains institutional

Make clear that permission does not transfer accountability to the team or system operating within the boundary.

The organisation remains responsible for:

- the purpose and boundary it approved;
- the adequacy of the controls;
- the information and resources provided;
- the conditions under which exceptions are handled;
- the monitoring and review process;
- responding when assumptions change;
- withdrawing permission when necessary.

Connect to Chapter 3's proposition that delegation does not transfer accountability without re-explaining the whole chapter.

The chapter should also distinguish permission from entitlement. A team does not acquire a permanent right to use a system because a pilot was approved. Permission may expire, narrow or be revoked.

### Movement 12 — The organisation can finally say yes

Return fully to the opening case.

The revised governance pathway should not end with the executive committee heroically approving the tool. The design change matters more than the individual decision.

The organisation creates a proportionate route under which:

- the use is classified according to work and consequence rather than the label *AI*;
- privacy, security and legal thresholds determine whether specialist review is needed;
- a named business owner may approve the bounded pilot within delegated authority;
- the conditions and prohibited actions are explicit;
- the evidence to be gathered is defined;
- exceptions and escalation have owners;
- the permission expires unless reviewed;
- material changes trigger reassessment.

The team can now proceed legitimately. The uncontrolled workaround becomes unnecessary and visible use returns to the organisation's governed environment.

Do not claim the process is automatically faster or that trust has increased. The chapter may state that clarity can create conditions for more confident action because people know the boundary and decision route.

### Movement 13 — Permission without evidence

The chapter must not end in triumph.

The organisation has defined permission, but important questions remain:

- Did users stay within the approved source set?
- Were the required source links retained?
- Did summaries omit or distort material information?
- Were exceptions escalated?
- Did the controls operate throughout the pilot?
- Did the system or repository change?
- Did the arrangement produce the expected benefit without unacceptable effects?

The approval record cannot answer these questions by itself.

End with:

> **Permission without evidence becomes optimism.**

The reader should feel that Chapter 9 — *Evidence Before Assurance* — is now unavoidable.

### Evidence establishes

- formalisation can enable or coerce depending on design and use;
- well-designed controls can support repair, transparency, flexibility and coordination;
- hierarchy and information use can erode enabling qualities;
- risk governance can be differentiated according to context and materiality;
- conditional approval, escalation, course correction and decommissioning are legitimate governance mechanisms;
- red tape and cumulative burden can weaken outcomes and encourage workarounds;
- disciplined baselines can coexist with adaptation and deference to expertise;

- governance should convert risk appetite into usable decision boundaries;
- permission can be a governance outcome;

## Red-team intellectual challenge

The first draft must survive the following challenge questions:

1. Does the argument quietly equate enabling governance with reduced governance?
2. Could the proposed proportionate pathway allow teams to understate material risk?
3. Does the chapter treat speed as evidence of good governance?
4. Are legal duties, rights and no-go decisions preserved?
5. Could conditional approval become indefinite use without proper review?
6. Are exceptions bounded, or are they merely documented workarounds?
7. Does deference to expertise bypass legitimate accountability?
8. Is the chapter assuming that local users always understand context better than central functions?
9. Could a clear permission boundary simply harden a poor or exclusionary decision?
10. Does the composite case unfairly caricature privacy, legal, security or risk functions?
11. Does the final pathway preserve independent challenge where consequence warrants it?
12. Is Governance as Permission distinguishable from Adler and Borys, Weill and Ross, NIST and ordinary risk management only through its integration into the book's wider architecture?

Any draft that fails these questions remains at first draft.

## Repetition and chapter-boundary controls

### Do not repeat Chapter 2

Do not re-explain the distinction between policy and control. Chapter 8 assumes that operational control is necessary and asks whether the control can produce a legitimate decision route.

### Do not repeat Chapter 4

Do not restate all conditions of meaningful accountability. Use only the idea that accountability requires practical conditions for judgement and action.

### Do not repeat Chapter 5

Do not reproduce the Delegated Authority Map in full. The chapter may refer to authority and boundaries but owns the organisational permission pathway, not the mapping of AI functions.

### Do not repeat Chapter 6

Do not revisit infrastructure domains at length. Access, workflow, logs and configuration appear only where needed to show that permission must be operationally enacted.

### Do not repeat Chapter 7

Do not return to training, literacy or calibrated reliance except to establish that capability is wasted where governance provides no legitimate room to act.

### Do not pre-empt Chapter 9

### Do not pre-empt Chapter 10
