## Three-Layer Evidence Test

### Evidence establishes

- formalisation may enable or coerce depending on design and use;
- controls can support repair, transparency, coordination and flexibility;
- hierarchy and information use can erode enabling qualities;
- risk governance can be differentiated according to context and materiality;
- conditional approval, course correction and withdrawal are legitimate governance mechanisms;
- cumulative administrative burden can weaken outcomes and encourage workarounds;
- disciplined baselines can coexist with adaptation and relevant expertise;
- responsible innovation requires responsiveness and has practical limits.

### Reasonable interpretation

- governance should translate risk appetite into usable decision boundaries;
- conditional permission can be stronger than either indefinite delay or unqualified approval;
- authorised exception pathways can strengthen control;
- escalation should connect uncertainty with relevant expertise and authority;
- governance burden should be tested against purpose and material risk;
- permission should remain monitored, reviewable and revocable.

### Gardner synthesis

- Governance as Permission;
- well-designed governance creates legitimate room to act;
- the opposite of weak governance is not necessarily more governance;
- permission to act is not permission to ignore risk;
- permission without evidence becomes optimism.

The three layers are now reconstructable from the manuscript without converting the chapter into academic prose.

## Contrary and limiting evidence survival

**PASS**

The revised manuscript preserves that:

- formal controls can become coercive through blame, surveillance or hierarchical use;
- proportionality can be gamed by understating risk;
- no-go remains a legitimate decision;
- conditional approval does not make every pilot appropriate;
- repeated exceptions may reveal a defective boundary or control erosion;
- escalation should not send every ambiguity to a central committee;
- demanding controls are not automatically red tape;
- responsible-innovation processes have power and resource limits;
- permission does not transfer institutional accountability;
- governance does not automatically increase speed, trust, adoption, productivity or innovation.
