# Chapter 4 — The Conditions for Meaningful Accountability

The manager had done everything the process required.

She had completed the training. Her name appeared in the approval record. The policy described her as the accountable decision-maker, and the workflow gave her the final choice: accept the recommendation, reject it or send the case for further review. The organisation could therefore say, with some confidence, that the decision remained human.

Then a difficult case arrived.

The recommendation looked clear enough, but something in it did not sit comfortably with her. The summary was concise, the risk rating was prominent and the preferred action had already been framed as the most defensible option. She wanted to know what information had been left out, how similar cases had been treated and whether the system was operating inside the conditions under which it had originally been approved.

The answers were harder to obtain than the decision itself.

The underlying evidence sat across several systems. The model limitations were documented in a technical file she had never seen. The business unit expected an answer before the end of the day. Sending the case for further review meant returning it to a specialist group reporting to the same executive sponsor who was pressing for faster decisions. She could reject the recommendation, but she could not pause the wider process or determine whether similar cases were still moving through it.

Formally, she had authority. Practically, the authority was thinner than it appeared.

Nothing in this situation requires a dramatic system failure. The AI may be performing well. The recommendation may even be correct. The governance problem lies elsewhere. The organisation has attached accountability to a human role without fully preserving the conditions that make the role usable.

This is the problem underneath the familiar assurance that a human remains in the loop. A person can be present, trained and named. They can possess a final approval button and still be poorly positioned to exercise independent judgement. The important question is therefore not whether the organisation can point to an accountable human. It is whether it has built the organisational conditions that allow that accountability to be exercised in practice.

Chapter 3 described the **Accountability–Control Gap**: the mismatch between the accountability assigned to a person, role or organisation and the practical control they can realistically exercise over a decision or outcome. That diagnosis matters because it exposes false comfort. But diagnosis alone is not enough. Leaders also need a way to examine what meaningful accountability requires before a name is placed beside a decision.

The evidence begins with an apparently simple point. Responsibility, ability, authority and control are related, but they are not the same. Human–automation research has long examined the need to keep them in a workable balance. Flemisch and colleagues argued that cooperative systems become unstable when responsibility exceeds the authority and ability available to the person expected to exercise control. Later work on meaningful human control applied that relationship directly to AI systems: assigning final authority to a human is insufficient if the person lacks the practical ability to use it (Flemisch et al., 2012; Cavalcante Siebert et al., 2023).

That distinction can be easy to overlook because organisations are accustomed to using role descriptions as evidence of governance. The role says who approves. The procedure says who can escalate. The system records who clicked the button. These arrangements matter, but they tell us only where responsibility has been placed. They do not tell us whether the person can understand the situation, see what matters, act with legitimate authority or change the outcome before the opportunity has passed.

This is where accountability has to be treated as an organisational capability rather than an attribution made after the event.

A useful starting point is **capability**. The person or arrangement carrying accountability must know enough about the work, the consequence and the system's role to form an independent judgement. That does not mean every executive, manager or professional must understand model architecture, training methods or every technical component. It means they must understand the decision they are being asked to own, what the AI has contributed to it, where the important limitations sit and what an alternative judgement would require.

This is practical evaluative competence. A manager reviewing an AI-supported employment decision needs enough understanding of the role, the evidence and the system's function to recognise when the recommendation is weak or inappropriate. A clinician using decision support needs domain expertise and knowledge of the tool's intended use and limitations. A procurement committee approving an agentic workflow needs access to specialist advice on permissions, data, failure modes and downstream actions. The capability may be distributed across a team, but it must be available to the accountable decision process when it is needed.

Capability can also erode quietly. As systems carry more of the routine work, people may have fewer opportunities to practise the judgement they are expected to supply when something unusual occurs. That risk is not inevitable. Automation can improve consistency, reduce workload and support better decisions in many contexts. Human-factors evidence is clear, however, that the effects depend on task design, reliability, workload, engagement and the environment in which intervention is required. The governance response should therefore not be to preserve manual work for its own sake. It should be to ask whether the organisation is maintaining the human capability on which its accountability model depends.

Training is part of the answer, but training records can become another reassuring artefact. A person may have completed a module without being able to recognise an out-of-domain case, challenge a plausible recommendation or formulate an alternative course of action. Current regulatory and governance guidance increasingly reflects this distinction. The EU AI Act requires people assigned to oversight of defined high-risk systems to have the necessary competence, training, authority and support. Australian guidance similarly expects accountable people to understand the system's capabilities, risks and behaviours and to be equipped to intervene appropriately (European Union, 2024; Australian Government, 2026).

Capability, however, is useless without **visibility**.

A highly experienced manager cannot judge evidence they never receive. A domain expert cannot recognise drift if the organisation does not monitor it. A reviewer cannot challenge the way a recommendation was formed if the interface presents only a conclusion and a confidence label. Visibility is therefore not the same as transparency in the abstract. It is access to the information that matters for this decision, in a form and at a time that allows the accountable person to use it.

Research on effective oversight describes this as epistemic access: the person must know enough about the situation, the risk and the available response to act effectively (Sterz et al., 2024). In organisational terms, that means the accountable role may need to see the evidence used, the important exclusions, the uncertainty attached to the output, the system's limits, signs that conditions have changed and the actions available if concern arises.

More information is not always better. A hundred-page technical report may satisfy a disclosure requirement while doing little to support a live decision. Explanations and confidence scores can help people calibrate reliance, but research does not show that they consistently improve human–AI performance. Information can be incomplete, overwhelming, poorly timed or persuasive in ways that increase rather than reduce dependence on the system (Lee & See, 2004; Zhang, Liao & Bellamy, 2020).

Visibility must therefore be designed around judgement. The question is not simply, *Was information available?* It is, *Could the accountable person identify what mattered, recognise when the case required challenge and understand what could be done next?*

That makes capability and visibility distinct. A person can have the expertise but not the evidence. They can have the evidence but not the expertise. Meaningful accountability requires the relationship between the two.

Even that relationship is insufficient without **authority**.

A person may understand the problem perfectly and still lack the legitimate standing to change the decision. This is particularly common where organisations place technical or professional experts in advisory roles while decision rights remain elsewhere. The expert can identify the weakness, explain the risk and recommend a pause, but the workflow, contract or hierarchy does not permit them to act.

Capability does not confer authority.

Authority has to be assigned. It includes the recognised right to question, defer, seek another view, override, escalate or stop. It also has to be protected. A policy can give a manager the right to reject an AI recommendation while performance measures punish delay, executive expectations favour acceptance or the organisation treats disagreement as evidence that the manager is resistant to innovation. On paper, the authority exists. In practice, it is costly or unsafe to use.

The distinction is visible in current governance frameworks. NIST separates responsibility, delegated authority, empowerment, training and resources rather than assuming they arrive together. The EU AI Act similarly distinguishes competence from authority and support. The OECD links accountability to role, context and ability to act. Across these sources, the underlying idea is consistent: accountability cannot be made meaningful merely by identifying a person. The organisation must define what that person is permitted to do and create conditions in which the permission can be exercised (NIST, 2023; OECD, 2024).

Authority, in turn, must be separated from **intervention capacity**.

A manager may have a legitimate right to stop a process but no usable way to do it. The request may require several approvals. The system may already have initiated downstream actions. The interface may allow rejection of one output without pausing the workflow that continues to generate others. The decision window may be shorter than the time needed to obtain specialist advice. By the time the concern reaches someone with technical control, the action may be difficult or impossible to reverse.

Authority is the right to act. Intervention capacity is the practical ability to make the action effective.

Sterz and colleagues use the idea of causal power: effective oversight requires a person to be able to make a relevant change, not merely observe what is happening (Sterz et al., 2024). Recent work on meaningful human authority and applied oversight develops the same point through temporal capacity, intervention power and intervention effectiveness. A person needs enough time, a usable mechanism and sufficient organisational support to alter the trajectory before avoidable harm becomes irreversible (Nitchals, 2026; van de Sande, Economou-Zavlanos & van Genderen, 2026).

A stop button may be part of that design, but its presence proves very little by itself. Does the person know when to use it? What exactly does it stop? Can the process be restarted safely? Does using it trigger an investigation, or merely an irritated phone call from the business unit? Can the decision be reversed after the system has acted? Is there an alternative way to continue the work if the AI is paused?

Intervention capacity depends on the whole setting around the person. It can include protected time, access to expertise, escalation response, system permissions, rollback mechanisms, staffing and the practical willingness of the organisation to tolerate interruption. In one setting, intervention may mean rejecting a recommendation. In another, it may mean suspending an automated workflow, restricting a model's permissions or removing the system from use. Meaningful accountability requires that the available intervention matches the role the AI is playing and the consequence that may follow.

The final condition is **accountability alignment**.

The manager in the opening scene might reasonably be accountable for considering the case and deciding whether the recommendation should be accepted. But should she also carry responsibility for evidence the system did not display, a vendor update she was not told about, a decision window imposed by senior leadership or the absence of a mechanism to suspend similar cases? Those conditions were shaped elsewhere.

AI-enabled decisions are organisational products. Providers, system owners, data owners, business leaders, risk functions, technical teams, reviewers and executives may each control different parts of the arrangement. Distributed responsibility is unavoidable. The governance task is to keep it legible.

Research on meaningful human control argues that responsibility attributed to a person should be commensurate with their ability and authority to control the system. The OECD's accountability principle similarly looks to role, context and ability to act. This does not mean that executives are responsible only for actions they directly perform. Their accountability may arise precisely because they authorised the system, allocated resources, accepted the risk or created the conditions under which others work. It does mean that the organisation should not load operational responsibility onto the nearest human while control over the relevant conditions sits elsewhere.

Accountability alignment therefore asks two questions at once. Is this person or role carrying responsibility proportionate to the control they can exercise? And is the wider chain of responsibility visible for the conditions beyond their control?

That brings the argument together.

The **Meaningful Accountability Test** is a practical organisational diagnostic for examining whether a person, role or organisation assigned accountability for an AI-mediated decision has the capability, visibility, authority and intervention capacity needed to exercise that accountability in practice, and whether the responsibility assigned is proportionate to the control that can realistically be exercised.

The component ideas are not new. They draw on established work in human factors, meaningful human control, effective oversight, organisational accountability and regulatory design. Several contemporary frameworks offer closely related conditions, including models of effective oversight and meaningful human authority. The contribution here is to gather that evidence into a concise organisational diagnostic, connect it to the Accountability–Control Gap and place it inside the wider operating system through which an organisation governs itself.

The test asks five questions.

**Capability:** Can the accountable arrangement understand the task, the system's role, the relevant risks and known limitations well enough to exercise independent judgement?

**Visibility:** Does it receive the timely and usable evidence needed to recognise when scrutiny, challenge or escalation is required?

**Authority:** Does it possess recognised and protected standing to question, defer, override, escalate or stop?

**Intervention capacity:** Can it act in time, through usable mechanisms and with sufficient support to alter the trajectory before avoidable harm becomes irreversible?

**Accountability alignment:** Is the responsibility assigned proportionate to the control that can realistically be exercised, while the wider chain of organisational responsibility remains visible?

These are not five boxes to tick or five scores to average. A serious weakness in one condition may make the whole arrangement fragile. A capable and well-informed manager without authority remains an adviser. A person with authority but no timely intervention mechanism becomes a ceremonial approver. A well-designed override available to someone who cannot understand when it is needed offers little protection. An organisation that satisfies the first four conditions but assigns responsibility for system-wide choices to a front-line reviewer still has an accountability problem.

The significance of any weakness depends on context. A low-consequence drafting tool does not require the same arrangement as a system affecting employment, safety, finance or essential services. The appropriate conditions will vary with uncertainty, autonomy, speed, reversibility and consequence. Nor must every capability sit inside one person. A governance team may collectively satisfy the test if expertise, information, decision rights and intervention mechanisms are brought together reliably and in time.

The test is also not an approval certificate. A system may preserve meaningful human accountability and still be unfair, insecure, ineffective, unnecessary or inappropriate. Human oversight can create false comfort when it is used to legitimise a system that should not have been deployed or to shift blame onto staff expected to catch problems they were never equipped to detect. Green's examination of government oversight policies makes that danger explicit, while Laux's work on institutionalised distrust reminds us that human overseers themselves are fallible and must be supported by institutional design rather than idealised as a final safety barrier (Green, 2022; Laux, 2023).

The proper use of the test is therefore diagnostic. Apply it to a defined decision or action. Identify who or what arrangement carries accountability. Examine what the AI contributes. Test the five conditions under ordinary, difficult and abnormal circumstances. Then identify which actor controls any limiting condition.

A weakness may require the organisation to strengthen the condition, redistribute authority or accountability, reduce the AI's role, slow the process, add an independent challenge path or decide that the use should not proceed. The value of the diagnostic lies less in the label it produces than in the organisational conversation it forces.

Return to the manager at the beginning of the chapter. She had training, formal authority and a final approval button. But the organisation had not given her adequate visibility, had constrained the practical use of her authority and had made her responsible for conditions she did not control. Her accountability was real in the administrative record and fragile in the work.

The organisation can now see what has to change if it wants her judgement to mean something.

But the test also reveals the other side of the arrangement. Every condition that must remain available to the human marks a boundary around what the system may do without them.

The next question is no longer simply who remains accountable.

It is:

**What authority has the organisation actually delegated to the AI?**
