## Purpose

Reconcile the first three chapters after the retrospective Nexus Evidence Engine backfill so that the opening book sequence uses one evidence standard, one provenance discipline and one controlled terminology system.

## Opening progression

The first three chapters now form a coherent sequence:

1. **Chapter 1 — Visibility of AI participation**  
   Organisations must see where AI participates in work, decisions and actions.

2. **Chapter 2 — Operational governance**  
   Policies and governance artefacts become meaningful only when coupled to authority, controls, evidence, monitoring and behaviour.

3. **Chapter 3 — Meaningful accountability**  
   Human accountability becomes fragile when practical control over AI-shaped decisions is insufficient.

The progression is cumulative rather than repetitive:

> **Visibility → Operational control → Accountability–control alignment**

This is consistent with the wider governance operating system while introducing only the elements each chapter requires.

## Claim continuity

### From Chapter 1 to Chapter 2

Chapter 1 establishes that product names and inventories do not reveal all governance-relevant uses. Chapter 2 then asks whether the organisation's policy and governance artefacts change what happens in those uses.

There is no contradiction between:

- Chapter 1's support for inventories; and
- Chapter 2's argument that documentation alone is insufficient.

Both chapters treat registers as necessary but incomplete.

### From Chapter 2 to Chapter 3

Chapter 2 ends by asking whether the accountable human retains practical control. Chapter 3 develops that question without repeating the policy-practice analysis.

There is no contradiction between:

- Chapter 2's focus on operationally enforceable control; and
- Chapter 3's recognition that not all meaningful control is technical.

Chapter 3 makes clear that control may also depend on information, understanding, authority, timing and organisational support.

### Proportionality across all three chapters

Chapter 1 establishes proportionality according to use and consequence. Chapters 2 and 3 preserve that boundary.

The manuscript does not imply that:

- every embedded AI feature is consequential;
- every use requires bespoke approval;
- every governance requirement must be technically enforced;
- every AI-supported decision requires the same form of human oversight;
- greater human involvement is always safer.

## Terminology reconciliation

| Term | Controlled meaning in Chapters 1–3 | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **AI use / use case** | AI capability applied to a defined organisational purpose, workflow or decision context | External/governance language |
| **Embedded AI** | AI capability situated within familiar software, infrastructure or workflows rather than presented as a separate project | Evidence-supported descriptive term |
| **Shadow AI** | AI use outside expected approval, monitoring or organisational control pathways | Evidence-supported; prevalence not universalised |
| **Decision participation** | The degree to which AI frames, informs, recommends, prioritises, decides, executes or adapts work | Gardner explanatory language |
| **Consequential use** | A use with material implications for people, rights, safety, money, access, employment, privacy or significant organisational interests | Gardner practical synthesis informed by risk guidance |
| **Governance artefact** | A visible policy, register, committee, form, standard, assessment or training module | Gardner descriptive category |
| **Governance capability** | The organisation's ability to use governance mechanisms to make, constrain, explain, monitor and revisit decisions | Gardner synthesis informed by ISO/NIST and implementation research |
| **Operational coupling** | Connection between stated intent and the roles, permissions, controls, evidence and behaviour that enact it | Gardner organisational interpretation informed by decoupling scholarship |
| **False assurance** | Confidence based on visible governance structure where operational effectiveness has not been demonstrated | Gardner diagnostic language |
| **Human oversight** | Human involvement that may range from nominal presence to effective understanding, evaluation, override and intervention | External/regulatory term; effectiveness must be specified |
| **Practical control** | What a person, role or organisation can realistically understand, challenge, change, stop or escalate in context and time | Gardner synthesis informed by human-control research |
| **Accountability–Control Gap** | Mismatch between assigned accountability and practical control realistically exercisable over a decision or outcome | Gardner original organisational diagnostic; provenance controlled |

## Contradiction and boundary reconciliation

The evidence backfill strengthens several boundaries:

1. **Visibility is not governance by itself.**
2. **Policy is not control by itself.**
3. **Human presence is not meaningful oversight by itself.**
4. **Inventories, policies and human roles remain necessary in many settings.**
5. **More process, more technical enforcement or more human touch is not automatically better.**
6. **Risk and appropriate governance vary by use, consequence, autonomy and context.**
7. **Distributed responsibility must remain legible rather than becoming diluted.**
8. **Original constructs are diagnostic syntheses, not externally validated universal models.**

No material contradiction requires reopening Chapters 1 or 2 or changing Chapter 3's central proposition.
