## Chapter role in the book

Chapter 9 ended with a limit that retrospective assurance could not resolve:

> **What happens when the system begins to act faster than the organisation can reconstruct?**

Chapter 10 completes the book by moving from retrospective evidence into governance while delegated action is being planned, handed off and executed.

The chapter moves the reader from:

> the organisation approved an agent, assigned an owner, restricted its tools and retained logs

To:

> **the organisation can determine, at each consequential step, which identity is acting, what authority applies now, which boundary is in force, what changed, what can still be interrupted or reversed, what evidence survives and who remains institutionally accountable.**

This is the final integration chapter. It does not introduce a new named framework, maturity model or technical architecture. Its contribution is to place the book's existing constructs under runtime pressure:

- the Meaningful Accountability Test;
- the Delegated Authority Map;
- governance infrastructure;
- Capability as Governance;
- Governance as Permission;
- the Evolved Governance Chain;
- authority drift;
- institutional accountability.

The chapter should leave the reader seeing agentic AI not as a separate futuristic topic, but as the point at which the book's central governance question becomes operationally unavoidable.

## Approved proposition package

### Dominant proposition

> **When AI can act, governance must be enforceable at the point where authority becomes action.**

### Supporting proposition

> **Delegated action should remain bounded, attributable, observable, interruptible and revocable, with reversibility or defined recourse proportionate to consequence.**

### Unit-of-analysis proposition

> **The relevant governance object is the complete action path connecting identity, authority, tools, state changes, counterparties, evidence and recourse — not the model or final output alone.**

These are Gardner book-level synthesis informed by current security, identity, human-control, assurance, provenance and agent-governance evidence. They are not technical standards, legal rules or externally validated definitions.

The phrase *action path* remains descriptive lower-case language and is not a new named construct.

## Reader entry belief

The reader may enter believing that:

- an approved agent remains governed because its purpose and owner are documented;
- authentication establishes entitlement to act;
- a restricted toolset contains the consequence;
- a human approval step preserves control;
- a kill switch solves loss of control;
- complete logs will make later assurance possible;
- individually tested agents form a safe multi-agent arrangement;
- pre-deployment evaluation remains valid after tools, models, permissions or counterparties change;
- existing AI governance can simply be extended to agents by adding more monitoring.

The chapter must unsettle these assumptions without implying that agentic AI is inherently uncontrollable or that prohibition is the only responsible response.

The reader should discover that the decisive issue is not whether a system is labelled an agent. It is whether delegated organisational authority remains legitimate, current, enforceable and recoverable while the system acts.

## Opening tension

Continue the composite knowledge-assistant case from Chapters 8 and 9 after controlled expansion.

The assistant has received one modest new function. When it finds a procedure with an identified owner and an approaching review date, it may:

- open a review task;
- assign it to the recorded owner;
- attach the procedure and source links;
- notify the owner's team.

The use appears bounded. The assistant cannot approve or publish a revised procedure, spend money or communicate outside the organisation. It uses a distinct service identity, accesses approved repositories and workflow tools, and records task creation.

During an ordinary run, it encounters two conflicting procedures. One has an outdated owner. The assistant queries another organisational agent for ownership and operational context. That agent returns a plausible answer based on a project directory and recent work patterns. The first agent opens several review tasks and assigns them to a manager who appears to own the process.

A downstream scheduling system treats the tasks as priority compliance work and reschedules operational activity. Notifications spread across several teams. One task enters a supplier workflow because the procedure includes a third-party dependency. None of this is catastrophic. Each action is permitted by some part of the environment. Yet nobody made one conscious decision to authorise the complete chain of consequence.

The organisation cannot answer quickly enough:

- which identity acted at each step;
- whose authority the system was exercising;
- whether the second agent could determine process ownership;
- whether the scheduling system could reinterpret a review task as operational priority;
- where fresh approval should have been required;
- which actions remain reversible;
- whether stopping the first agent would leave damaging partial state;
- what evidence survives across the handoffs.

Opening question:

> **At what point did permission become action that nobody could still meaningfully interrupt?**

The opening must remain ordinary and plausible. Do not manufacture a spectacular failure, cyberattack or sentient system. The tension comes from individually reasonable permissions combining into a consequence no single decision-maker consciously authorised.

## Primary reader discovery

The reader should discover that agentic governance is not merely stronger pre-deployment review or faster monitoring. It is the preservation of accountable authority while action is being planned, delegated and executed.

By the end of the chapter, the reader should stop asking only:

> Is the agent approved and monitored?

and begin asking:

> **At this action, under these conditions, which identity is acting, what authority applies, what boundary is enforceable, what can still be interrupted or reversed, and who remains accountable for the arrangement?**

## Terminology and boundaries

### Agentic AI

Treat agentic AI as a spectrum involving different combinations of planning, tool use, state, persistence, delegation, coordination and reduced direct supervision. Do not depend on one universal autonomy threshold.

### Agent

Use *agent* for a software system that can pursue a task through multiple steps and interact with tools, services or other agents with some delegated discretion. Do not imply personhood, consciousness or moral agency.

### Identity and authorisation

Identity establishes which human, service, application or agent is requesting action. Authorisation determines whether that identified actor may perform a specific action on a specific resource, through a particular tool, for a legitimate purpose under current conditions.

### Runtime control

Use *runtime control* descriptively for controls operating while a system plans, requests or performs action. Some runtime controls are technical; others involve human or institutional decision points.

### Intervention

Intervention may include refusal, step-up approval, pause, cancellation, quarantine, credential revocation, containment, rollback, fallback, safe degradation, decommissioning, remedy or compensation.

### Reversibility and recourse

Reversibility asks whether an action can be undone without unacceptable harm. Recourse asks what remedy remains when it cannot.

### Observability

Observability means preserving enough information about identity, authority, tools, requests, handoffs, state changes and outcomes for live intervention and later reconstruction. It does not require indefinite retention of every token, hidden state or chain-of-thought.

### Adaptive governance

Adaptive governance means reassessing permission, controls, evidence and intervention when capability, tools, counterparties, context or consequence changes materially. It does not mean allowing an AI system to expand its own authority or rewrite its own governance rules.

## Narrative movement

### 1. One modest action

Open with the expanded assistant and show why the task-creation permission appears reasonable. The organisation has a purpose, distinct identity, restricted repositories, bounded workflow tool, human owner and task-creation logs. The case should not feel like obvious negligence.

Let the consequence widen through the second agent and scheduling system before explaining the theory.

### 2. Agentic is not binary

Use OECD and current official guidance to establish differences in planning, tool use, persistence, delegation, coordination and supervision.

State:

> **The governance significance of an agent lies in what it can cause to happen, not in whether it is marketed as autonomous.**

Preserve that bounded delegated action can produce legitimate value.

### 3. Output becomes action

Show the movement from information to recommendation, tool call, state change, delegation and downstream consequence. The material change is not abstract intelligence; it is the attachment of tools, credentials and pathways through which outputs alter work.

Introduce the action-path proposition only after the reader sees the whole sequence.

### 4. Identity is not authority

Return to the second agent's ownership answer. Both agents were authenticated; that did not establish authority to determine ownership or rely on that answer for consequential assignment.

Use NIST identity work, allied cyber guidance and protocol specifications to support distinct machine identity, principal binding, minimum privilege, expiring delegation, action-level authorisation, role separation and grant-chain records.

State:

> **Identity establishes who or what is requesting action. Authorisation determines whether that action is permitted in the present context.**

### 5. Capability still does not confer authority

Bring back the approved Chapter 5 proposition under runtime conditions:

> **Capability does not confer authority.**

A tool description or discoverable capability can show what is technically available without deciding what is legitimate now. A2A and MCP can carry identity, requests, task state and authorisation prompts; they do not determine institutional purpose, risk acceptance or remedy.

### 6. Governance at execution

Now state the dominant proposition:

> **When AI can act, governance must be enforceable at the point where authority becomes action.**

Develop it through action-level checks, scoped identities, restricted tools, sandboxing, rate or transaction limits, checkpoints, step-up approval, egress controls and fail-closed behaviour.

Preserve that not every organisational judgement can or should be encoded as machine policy.

### 7. Human approval without ritual

Ask where human approval should have occurred. Do not answer that every step needs approval. Approval points should reflect consequence, uncertainty, reversibility, time, affected parties and whether authority crosses a material boundary.

State:

> **Human approval is strongest when it occurs before a consequential action and the reviewer has the information, time and authority to change the outcome.**

### 8. Intervention is plural

Stopping the first agent may not recall tasks, restore schedules, close supplier workflows or correct notifications. Show why one universal kill switch is too simple.

Develop refusal, pause, cancellation, credential revocation, narrowing of access, quarantine, checkpoint return, rollback, fallback, containment, remedy and safe decommissioning.

State the supporting proposition.

### 9. Evidence travels with action

Connect to Chapter 9 without repeating its assurance lesson. The organisation needs enough evidence to identify the acting identity, principal, permission, tool, state change, handoff, approval, result and available intervention.

The Evolved Governance Chain must now capture material events quickly enough for intervention and later evaluation. Do not equate chain-of-thought with reliable explanation.

### 10. Observability can fail through volume

Use UK AISI work to show how longer tasks, tool use, delegation and memory can create trace overload. More logs may reduce oversight when material events are not distinguishable.

The answer is designed observability around material authority and consequence, not complete transparency.

### 11. Multi-agent safety is a system property

Return to the assistant, ownership agent, scheduling system and supplier workflow. Each component may obey its local rule while the complete arrangement produces an unauthorised consequence.

State:

> **The safety of a multi-agent arrangement cannot be inferred by adding together the safety claims of its components.**

Distinguish singular, federated and open arrangements, and governance through control, influence, dependency and accepted residual risk.

### 12. Evaluation is a gate

Use AgentDojo, ToolEmu, Agent Security Bench and *AI Agents That Matter* to show both the value and limits of evaluation.

State:

> **Pre-deployment evaluation is a gate, not a permanent assurance conclusion.**

Re-evaluate after changes in models, tools, permissions, prompts, data, memory, counterpart agents, integrations, context or consequence.

### 13. Adaptive governance without self-government

Show how an initially legitimate permission becomes inadequate when tools, volume, counterparties, cross-boundary use or consequence changes. Adaptive governance means institutional reassessment, not an agent rewriting its own permissions.

### 14. Accountability after delegation

Return to:

> **Delegation does not transfer accountability.**

The agent can execute authority but cannot absorb institutional responsibility for purpose, permission, tools, data, intervention, evidence, counterparties, remedy or withdrawal.

### 15. Close the book

Return to the question first made explicit in Chapter 3: what happens when a person or institution remains accountable for action it is progressively less able to understand, challenge or control?

The answer is not prohibition, a symbolic human at the end or another policy statement. It is organisational design that keeps authority, capability, visibility, evidence, intervention and accountability aligned while intelligence and action are distributed.

Bring together the four book propositions sparingly:

- capability does not confer authority;
- delegation does not transfer accountability;
- successful execution does not eliminate the need for assurance;
- when AI can act, governance must be enforceable where authority becomes action.

Final movement:

> **The organisation must still be able to govern what it permits AI to do.**
