## Register purpose

Control the material claims supporting Chapter 7 and preserve the distinction among:

- verified external evidence;
- reasonable organisational interpretation;
- Gardner book-level synthesis;

## Evidence gaps and monitored propositions

### Long-term expertise effects

Longitudinal cross-sector evidence on the effect of generative AI on professional expertise, skill atrophy and the development of novice judgement remains limited.

**Control:** use conditional language and retain both augmentation and erosion pathways.

### Manager-specific governance capability

There is strong authoritative support for role-specific manager capability but less mature empirical evidence on which development methods produce reliable governance behaviour across sectors.

**Control:** frame the manager-capability proposition as a practical governance requirement supported by guidance and adjacent organisational evidence, not a settled competency taxonomy.

### Capability assessment

No universal validated instrument currently establishes organisational AI-governance capability across roles, systems and contexts.

**Control:** do not introduce a scoring model or certification claim. Use proportionate examples of performance evidence.

### Intervention behaviour

Evidence connecting AI training directly to intervention quality in consequential live workflows is still developing.

**Control:** distinguish evidence-supported design principles from claims of proven intervention effectiveness.

## Three-Layer Evidence Summary

### External evidence establishes

- AI literacy includes understanding, use, critical evaluation and ethics;
- training completion and learning transfer are different outcomes;
- reliance on AI should be calibrated and is affected by context;
- human-AI collaboration is not automatically synergistic;
- AI can improve performance, particularly for less experienced workers in some settings;
- generative AI can shift cognitive effort and may reduce reported scrutiny under high confidence;
- organisational readiness depends on more than technology;
- psychological safety, leader openness and voice mechanisms affect challenge and learning;
- regulation and guidance require role-appropriate literacy, competence, authority, support and escalation.

### Reasonable organisational interpretation

- capability is role-specific, situated and often collective;
- organisations should preserve the judgement on which their accountability model depends;
- training should be practised and tested in realistic work conditions;
- managers need to govern human-AI work allocation and intervention, not merely use tools;
- challenge requires knowledge, voice, channels, authority and organisational response.

### Gardner synthesis

> **Capability is not merely preparation for AI governance. It is one of the mechanisms through which governance operates.**

> **Compliance can show that training occurred. Capability asks whether judgement can still occur when it matters.**

## Monitoring triggers

Review the register if:

- NIST publishes a revised AI RMF affecting workforce capability, roles, culture or oversight;
- EU guidance materially changes Article 4 AI-literacy expectations;
- Australian mandatory AI competency or literacy obligations emerge;
- stronger longitudinal studies establish durable expertise or deskilling effects;
- validated organisational measures of AI-governance capability become available;
- robust cross-sector evidence links training design to real intervention behaviour.
