## Approved chapter argument

The chapter establishes that, once AI can initiate action through tools, services, records and other agents, governance must operate close enough to execution to preserve legitimate authority, intervention, evidence and institutional accountability.

Approved governing propositions:

> **When AI can act, governance must be enforceable at the point where authority becomes action.**

> **Delegated action should remain bounded, attributable, observable, interruptible and revocable, with reversibility or defined recourse proportionate to consequence.**

> **The relevant governance object is the complete action path connecting identity, authority, tools, state changes, counterparties, evidence and recourse — not the model or final output alone.**

Approved whole-book closing proposition:

> **The organisation must still be able to govern what it permits AI to do.**

## Approved provenance position

No new named Chapter 10 framework is authorised.

The chapter may integrate established work on:

- agent identity, authentication and contextual authorisation;
- least privilege, expiring delegation and principal binding;
- action-level controls, restricted tools, sandboxing and policy enforcement;
- meaningful human control and selective approval;
- pause, cancellation, revocation, rollback, fallback, containment, decommissioning and recourse;
- action evidence, provenance, auditability and observability;
- multi-agent interaction and system-level risk;
- runtime assurance and continuous monitoring;
- benchmark and evaluation limits;
- adaptive, lifecycle and change-triggered governance.

The phrase *action path* remains descriptive lower-case wording. It is not a named Gardner construct.

The chapter does not present the runtime-action synthesis as:

- the first theory of agentic governance;
- a universal technical architecture;
- a runtime-security or assurance standard;
- a maturity model, compliance score or certification method;
- proof that a system is safe, lawful, fair, trustworthy or effective;
- a requirement for complete observability or indefinite trace retention;
- a universal human-approval or kill-switch prescription;
- authority for an AI system to rewrite its own permissions;
- a transfer of institutional accountability to an agent.

## Approved intellectual boundaries

The chapter preserves that:

- agentic AI is a spectrum rather than a binary category;
- many risks are extensions of established automation, identity and cybersecurity problems;
- bounded delegated action can create legitimate organisational value;
- identity and technical capability do not establish current authority;
- protocol compliance does not confer institutional legitimacy;
- prompts and model guardrails are not substitutes for action-level control;
- human approval can become ceremonial where reviewers lack evidence, time, authority or alternatives;
- intervention includes prevention, interruption, recovery, rollback, fallback and recourse;
- complete observability is unrealistic and may create privacy, security and retention risk;
- chain-of-thought is not complete or reliable evidence of action;
- multi-agent arrangements require system-level governance;
- benchmark findings remain bounded to tested settings;
- pre-deployment evaluation is not permanent assurance;
- adaptive governance remains an institutional process;
- delegation does not transfer accountability.
