# Forensic Research Brief 02 — Authority, Judgement, Capability, Intervention and Accountability

**Date:** 2026-11-01  
**Materiality:** M3 — core publication argument and original-framework control  
**Status:** Evidence supports progression to framework design, subject to Alec Gardner approval

## Research question

What conditions make human oversight of AI genuinely meaningful rather than merely procedural, and can those conditions support a distinctive Nexus BMG framework for organisational AI governance?

## Concise synthesis

The evidence strongly supports the book's central concern but also shows that the underlying problem is not new. Human-factors, meaningful-human-control and accountability research already establish that responsibility becomes problematic when a human lacks the ability, authority, situational awareness or practical intervention capacity to control an automated system.

The strongest original territory therefore lies in **organisational synthesis and application**, not claiming discovery of the underlying principle.

A defensible Gardner contribution is to turn established research into a practical governance test for AI-enabled work:

> Does the person or role held accountable retain the capability, information, time, authority and practical intervention capacity needed to exercise meaningful judgement?

If not, the organisation has an **accountability–control gap**.

## Evidence by dimension

### 1. Authority

Australian AI safety guidance states that accountable people should have sufficient authority to oversee and intervene effectively across the AI lifecycle. Meaningful-human-control scholarship similarly argues that responsibility should not exceed authority and ability.

**Evidence position:** strong.

### 2. Judgement

Recent 2026 research distinguishes genuine evaluative agency from rubber-stamp approval. A human presence alone does not establish meaningful oversight.

**Evidence position:** strong.

### 3. Capability

Oversight depends on competence, system understanding, information access and retained professional skill. Longstanding automation research documents the out-of-the-loop performance problem, including degraded situation awareness and slower recovery when automation fails.

Recent AI research extends this concern to skill atrophy and cognitive dependency.

**Evidence position:** strong, but effects are context-dependent.

### 4. Intervention

Meaningful oversight requires actual mechanisms to pause, override, roll back, revise or shut down AI action. Modern Australian guidance explicitly requires intervention points proportionate to autonomy and stakes.

**Evidence position:** very strong.

### 5. Accountability

Elish's moral-crumple-zone concept shows the danger of assigning blame to the nearest human even where that person had limited practical control. Current responsibility literature continues to treat this as a serious organisational and governance risk.

**Evidence position:** strong.

## Important boundary condition

The answer is **not** “put a human in every loop”.

Current guidance and scholarship explicitly recognise that excessive synchronous review can create bottlenecks, undermine useful autonomy and even degrade performance. Meaningful control can be distributed across:

- design-time constraints;
- pre-authorised operating boundaries;
- runtime monitoring;
- escalation triggers;
- intervention mechanisms;
- post-event assurance and accountability.

Therefore the book should distinguish:

**human approval** from **human control**, and  
**human presence** from **human agency**.

## Red-team findings

Four challenges must remain visible:

1. A competent human can still become overloaded by AI output volume.
2. A person may formally possess authority but lack enough information or time to exercise it.
3. Increasing human intervention can sometimes reduce performance and create unnecessary friction.
4. Responsibility in complex AI systems may legitimately be distributed across designers, deployers, managers and operators rather than concentrated in one “accountable owner”.

## Proposed Gardner framework — working version

### Meaningful Accountability Test

For each consequential AI-enabled decision or action, test five conditions:

1. **Capability** — Does the accountable human understand the task, system limits and relevant risks well enough to judge?
2. **Visibility** — Do they receive the information and evidence needed to know when scrutiny or intervention is required?
3. **Authority** — Are they empowered to question, defer, override, escalate or stop?
4. **Intervention capacity** — Can they actually act in time, with usable mechanisms and organisational support?
5. **Accountability alignment** — Is the responsibility assigned to them proportionate to the control they can realistically exercise?

Working diagnostic:

> **Accountability should not outrun capability, visibility, authority or intervention capacity.**

This is a Gardner synthesis informed by prior meaningful-human-control and human-factors scholarship; it must not be presented as though the underlying relationships were newly discovered.

## Book-level proposition strengthened by evidence

> **Accountability without meaningful control risks becoming governance theatre.**

This is suitable as a provocative Gardner synthesis if the book immediately grounds it in the evidence on meaningful human control, out-of-the-loop performance and moral crumple zones.

A more academically defensible formulation is:

> **Formal accountability becomes fragile when the person held responsible lacks the capability, information, authority or practical means to exercise meaningful control.**

## Recommended architecture implication

The accountability problem should move earlier in the book and become the hinge between “why policy is insufficient” and “how governance must be designed”.

The likely narrative sequence is now:

1. AI is already embedded in work.
2. Policy does not equal governance.
3. Accountability can remain human while control quietly migrates elsewhere.
4. Meaningful accountability requires alignment of capability, visibility, authority and intervention.
5. Governance must then be designed around delegated authority, evidence and control across the lifecycle.
